# A bridge grocery benefit for households just above the SNAP line

## Summary

Nearly half of people who are food insecure have incomes too high for SNAP, so they fall between
benefits and food banks. A state or local "bridge" benefit would give those households a reduced monthly
grocery credit, phased out as pay rises, so a small raise doesn't mean a sudden loss of food help.
This is a proposal; we found no measured results for this exact design.

## How it works

- A state, county or regional funder sets an income band above the SNAP limit, for example up to a set
  percent above it, and enrolls households through the same application used for SNAP or free school
  meals.
- Eligible households get a monthly credit on a card limited to groceries. The credit falls
  step by step as income rises, to avoid a cliff.
- What's new: SNAP, WIC and school meals stop at fixed income lines, and food banks fill the gap without
  a dependable amount. A phased benefit makes that gap predictable and pairs it with benefits people
  already apply for.
- Why it could work: Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap finds the national average meal costs $3.70
  and the national food budget shortfall surpassed $33 billion, so the need is large and many
  households sit outside SNAP's income limit.
- Test it first: one county pilot with a comparison group, measuring food insufficiency, grocery debt
  and food bank use after six and twelve months.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** A state or county can fund a pilot from general funds or a food assistance line, and
  use its existing SNAP or school-meal eligibility systems to enroll families.
- **People and companies with means:** Philanthropists and employers can fund the first county pilot
  and its evaluation, and employers can add a grocery stipend for lower-paid staff. Grocers can
  offer a bridge-card discount at checkout.
- **You, this week:** While no such program exists in most places, find the help you do qualify for: ask
  your local food bank, call 211 and ask your school about free or reduced-price meals. Ask your county
  or state representative whether a pilot like this could be tried.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps working households just above the SNAP income line, who can still miss meals. Households with
  children are the clearest fit, since 18.4% of them were food insecure in 2024.
- Cost is the benefit itself plus staff. For scale, Map the Meal Gap's lower-bound estimate of the
  national food budget shortfall is over $33 billion a year for all food-insecure people; a bridge
  benefit would cover only part of that, and the cost depends on the band chosen.
- Paid by the state, county or private funders. Could start as a pilot within a year.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Costs rise as the income band widens, and a fixed budget may mean a waiting list.
- A new benefit adds paperwork, and phase-outs can still create a cliff if steps are too steep.
- It treats the shortfall, not the price of food, so it doesn't lower what groceries cost.
