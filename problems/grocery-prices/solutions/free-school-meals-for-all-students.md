# Free school breakfast and lunch for every student (Community Eligibility)

## Summary

The federal Community Eligibility Provision (CEP) lets high-need schools serve breakfast and lunch free
to every student, with no meal applications. For a family with children, that removes two meals a day
from the grocery bill on school days. About 27.6 million students now attend a CEP school.

## How it works

- A school, a group of schools or a whole district qualifies if at least 25% of its students are
  "identified students": children already certified for free meals through programs such as SNAP,
  Medicaid, Head Start, foster care or homelessness status.
- Instead of collecting applications, the school is reimbursed based on its share of identified
  students.
- In 2025-26, 55,362 schools use CEP, a record. That is 74.9% of schools that are eligible, which leaves
  about a quarter of eligible schools not using it.
- Growth was the slowest in the program's history. Seventeen states and D.C. had fewer CEP schools than
  the year before, because of falling enrollment, school closures, falling identified-student shares and
  federal reimbursement that doesn't cover costs.
- Growth in recent years came from "direct certification" with Medicaid data, which 44 states now use.
- Some states go further. New York offers universal free meals in all participating schools in 2025-26,
  and California gives every student one free breakfast and one free lunch each school day.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States can pay the gap between federal reimbursement and meal costs, adopt Medicaid
  direct certification and fund universal meals as New York and California do. Congress can adjust
  reimbursement so schools can stay in the program. School boards can decide to join.
- **People and companies with means:** Local foundations and employers can pay off student meal debt and
  cover the short-term cost of a district joining. Grocers and food companies can donate to school
  pantries and weekend food bags.
- **You, this week:** Ask the school office or district nutrition page whether your child's school
  offers free meals for all, and if not, whether you qualify for free or reduced-price meals and how
  to apply. Parents can also ask the school board whether the district has looked at joining CEP.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps all students, especially children in families just above the free-meal income line, who often
  owe money for meals or skip them.
- Cost depends on the district: the federal government reimburses based on identified students, and the
  state or district covers the rest. Exact per-district costs weren't found in the sources; a district
  nutrition office can estimate them.
- Takes effect at the start of a school year or term once a district opts in.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Schools with only slightly more than 25% identified students are reimbursed for fewer meals than are
  served, so they may lose money.
- The 2025 budget law cut SNAP, which FRAC says could reduce direct certification and threaten schools'
  ability to qualify for and keep CEP.
- Free meals cover school days only; weekends, holidays and summer remain gaps.
