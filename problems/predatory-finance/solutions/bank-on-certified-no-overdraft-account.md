# Switch to a Bank On certified account with no overdraft fees, and take back control of auto-debits

## Summary

Bank On certified accounts are low-cost checking accounts from regular banks and credit unions that
meet national standards, including **no overdraft or non-sufficient-funds fees** and a monthly fee of
$5 or less. Over 14 million were open and active at the end of 2024. For someone losing $35 at a time to
overdrafts, switching can stop the drain this week, and pairing it with the federal right to revoke
automatic debits puts the person, not the lender, in charge of when money leaves.

## How it works

The Cities for Financial Empowerment Fund sets the Bank On National Account Standards (2025-2026).
A certified account must have:

- no overdraft or NSF fees, and no activation, closure, dormancy, inactivity or low-balance fees
- an opening deposit of $25 or less
- a monthly fee of $5 or less, or $10 or less if it can be waived with a single transaction such as a
  direct deposit
- a free debit card, free in-network ATMs, free direct deposit, and free bill pay or at least four free
  money orders or cashier's checks a month
- federal deposit insurance

If a payment would overdraw the account, it is declined instead of paid with a fee. The standards also
recommend accepting ITINs and municipal or consular IDs, and denying new customers only for actual past
fraud, which helps people shut out of banking before.

The second half is control. The CFPB says you can withdraw permission for a company to take automatic
payments: tell the company, tell your bank in writing, consider a stop-payment order, keep records, and
report any debit taken after you revoked it so you can dispute it. Stopping the debit does not cancel
the debt, so you still need to pay another way, but you choose the timing.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Local Bank On coalitions are partnerships between local public officials, government
  agencies, financial institutions and community groups. A city or county without one can start one. A city or state could pay benefits, tax refunds and
  payroll for public employees into certified accounts by default when people don't name another
  account. Regulators can keep publishing overdraft data so people can compare.
- **People and companies with means:** Banks and credit unions can certify an account (the list is
  public) and report results to the St. Louis Fed's Bank On data hub. Employers can show certified
  options during onboarding next to the direct deposit form. Philanthropists can fund local Bank On
  coalitions and the free financial coaching that helps people switch. Fintech founders can build
  low-balance alerts and "move this bill date" tools that work with these accounts.
- **You, this week:** Open the list of certified accounts at https://joinbankon.org/accounts/, find
  your state, and pick a bank or credit union near you. Open the account, move your direct deposit, and
  then use the CFPB steps to move or revoke automatic debits from your old account, especially loan
  payments, so one auto-debit can't wipe out your grocery money again.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps anyone paying overdraft fees, which is 12% of adults with a bank account in 2025, and the 6%
of adults who have no bank account at all. The cost to the customer is at most $5 a month, often $0.
Banks pay to offer and certify the accounts and give up overdraft revenue on them. It works as soon as
the account is open and deposits are moved over.

## Trade-offs and risks

- A declined payment can still bring a late fee from the company you owe. A no-overdraft account stops
  the bank's fee, not the biller's.
- Some people rely on overdraft as short-term credit. Without it, they need another backstop, such as a
  credit union small-dollar loan.
- Moving direct deposit and automatic bills takes some effort, and old debits can hit the old account
  if it isn't closed carefully.
- Certification sets a floor. Compare ATM networks and branch locations before choosing.
