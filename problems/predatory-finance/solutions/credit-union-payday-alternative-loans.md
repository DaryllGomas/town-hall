# Use a credit union payday alternative loan (PAL) instead of a payday loan

## Summary

Federal credit unions are allowed to make small "payday alternative loans" of up to $2,000, capped at
28% annual interest plus an application fee of no more than $20, repaid in installments over up to a
year, with no rollovers. A payday loan averages about $375 and costs the typical borrower about $520 in
interest a year because people keep re-borrowing. A PAL is a cheaper way to cover the same gap.

## How it works

The rules are in federal regulation (12 CFR 701.21):

- **PAL I:** $200 to $1,000, repaid over one to six months. You must have been a member of the credit
  union for at least one month.
- **PAL II:** up to $2,000, repaid over one to twelve months. The PAL II rule does not include the
  one-month membership wait, and the credit union may not charge overdraft or NSF fees in connection with
  the loan.
- **Both:** the interest rate can be up to 1,000 basis points above the credit union's normal ceiling.
  The normal ceiling is 18% through September 10, 2027, so PALs top out at 28%. The application fee
  must reflect actual cost and can't exceed $20. The loan must be fully paid down in installments, there
  are no rollovers, a borrower can have only one PAL at a time and no more than three in six months.

A person joins a federal credit union, asks whether it offers PALs, applies, and repays in fixed
installments, often by automatic transfer from the same account.

Example of the difference: a $400 loan at 28% repaid over three months costs roughly $20 in interest,
plus up to $20 in fees. Pew found that in Ohio before its 2018 reform, owing $300 for five months
typically cost $680 in fees and interest.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** The NCUA already allows PALs. State credit union regulators could adopt the same
  terms for state-chartered credit unions where they don't already, and states could fund loan-loss
  reserves so small credit unions can afford to offer PALs without risking their capital.
- **People and companies with means:** Philanthropists and community foundations can fund loan-loss
  reserves or deposits at local credit unions, especially community development credit unions, so they
  can make more PALs. Employers can partner with a credit union so employees can join through work and
  repay by payroll deduction. Fintech builders can give credit unions cheap, fast underwriting and
  same-day funding so a PAL is as quick as a payday storefront.
- **You, this week:** Use the NCUA's Credit Union Locator (https://mapping.ncua.gov/) to find federal
  credit unions near you and check which ones you can join (many accept anyone who lives or works in an
  area). Call and ask: "Do you offer payday alternative loans, PAL I or PAL II, and how fast can I get
  one?" Join now, even before you need it, so the PAL I one-month wait is behind you.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps anyone who can join a federal credit union and needs a few hundred to $2,000. The borrower
pays interest of up to 28% and a fee of up to $20, instead of payday costs that often total more than
the amount borrowed. Credit unions carry the default risk; the rule limits their total PAL lending to
20% of net worth. It works the same day a credit union approves the loan.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Not every credit union offers PALs. They are optional, and the PAL rules apply to federal credit
  unions only.
- Credit unions underwrite the loan, so some applicants will be turned down. Someone in a crisis today
  may not get a PAL fast enough.
- A 28% loan is still debt. If the shortfall is ongoing, a loan only delays it.
- The 18% base ceiling is temporary. If the NCUA lets it lapse, the PAL cap would fall with it, which
  could make PALs less attractive for credit unions to offer.
