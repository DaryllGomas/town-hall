# Give every U.S. bank customer a one-tap gambling block with a 48-hour wait to turn it off

## Summary

Banks in the UK, Ireland and Australia let customers switch on a block that declines card payments to
gambling merchants, and some make you wait 48 hours before the block can be lifted. That delay is the
point: it gives a person a cooling-off window against a late-night urge to chase losses. We could not
find a U.S. bank or credit union that advertises this, even though online sports betting is now
linked to more bankruptcies and debt in collections in the states that allow it. The idea: U.S. banks,
credit unions and card issuers offer the same block, by default in their apps.

## How it works

What exists elsewhere:

- **NatWest (UK)** lets debit card customers turn on a gambling block in its app. Gambling payments are
  declined, and after a customer removes the block they must wait 48 hours before gambling payments go
  through. Starling Bank added a similar 48-hour delay.
- **Bank of Ireland** started voluntary gambling blocks on debit cards in 2025; its announcement says
  99% of gambling transactions were funded by debit cards.
- **Bank of Melbourne (Australia)** blocks card authorizations from merchants registered under the
  "Betting/Casino Gambling" merchant category code; removing the block can take up to two business
  days.

What the U.S. version would do:

1. A bank, credit union or card issuer adds a "Block gambling" switch to card controls in its app. It
   declines card payments to merchants registered under the betting and casino gambling merchant
   category code, the same signal Bank of Melbourne uses.
2. Turning it on is instant. Turning it off starts a 48-hour wait.
3. Optional extras the customer can choose: also block transfers to known betting operators and crypto
   exchanges, and send a trusted person a notice if the block is removed.
4. Issuers report how many customers use it, so the effect can be studied.

What's new here is doing it in the U.S. at scale, plus the optional extensions beyond card payments.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** State gaming regulators could require licensed sportsbooks to keep using correct
  merchant codes so bank blocks work, and to honor a customer's bank block instead of steering them to
  other payment methods. State and federal bank regulators could make clear that offering the block is
  a consumer-protection feature they welcome.
- **People and companies with means:** Large banks, card networks and credit unions can build this in
  one product cycle; the payment codes already exist. Fintech founders can offer it to small banks and
  credit unions as a ready-made card-control module. Philanthropists and researchers can fund a
  randomized study with a willing bank to measure whether blocks reduce overdrafts, missed payments
  and gambling losses.
- **You, this week:** If betting is draining you or someone you love, call or text the National
  Problem Gambling Helpline at 1-800-MY-RESET, or use its online chat, to reach help in your state.
  Then call your bank or credit union and ask whether it can block gambling merchants on your card. If
  it can't, ask them to add the feature and consider moving to a bank that will.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps people who gamble more than they want to and the families who depend on the same account.
The UCLA-reviewed study found that in states with online sports betting, the likelihood of personal
bankruptcy rose 25% to 30% after legalization and average collection amounts rose 8%. Cost to banks is
a software feature and customer support; cost to customers is zero. It works the moment it is turned
on.

## Trade-offs and risks

- A determined person can get around it with another bank, cash, or a payment method that isn't coded
  as gambling. It adds friction, not a wall.
- Merchant codes miss some gambling: Bank of Melbourne notes that merchants whose main business is
  something else (such as shops selling lottery tickets) aren't caught, and recurring payments already
  authorized may still go through.
- Gambling companies may push other payment routes, which is why the regulator piece matters.
- Building and supporting the feature costs money, and some banks may see little reason to do it
  without customer demand.
- There is limited published evidence so far on how much these blocks reduce losses. A U.S. pilot
  should measure that, not assume it.
