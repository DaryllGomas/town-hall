# Pass state small-loan rules like Ohio's 2018 law, with low rate caps and affordable installments

## Summary

Payday and title loans are regulated mostly by states, and federal protections are uncertain: the
CFPB's remaining payday-loan payment rule took effect in March 2025 but the agency said it would not
prioritize enforcing it and plans to reconsider it. States can act on their own. Ohio's 2018 Fairness
in Lending Act turned two-week balloon loans into short installment loans with hard caps on total
cost, and state officials estimated it would save Ohio consumers $75 million a year.

## How it works

Before reform, Pew found that owing $300 for five months in Ohio typically cost $680 in fees and
interest, versus $172 in Colorado, which had reformed in 2010. Ohio's 2018 law (H.B. 123), as Pew
describes it:

- interest of up to 28% a year, plus a monthly fee of up to 10% of the original loan, capped at $30
- total interest and fees capped at 60% of the amount borrowed, so the debt can't grow without end
- at least three months to repay, unless each monthly payment is limited to 6% of the borrower's gross
  monthly income
- result: a $400, three-month loan can't cost more than $109

A state that wants to do the same would: (1) pass a law setting an all-in cost cap and minimum
installment terms for loans under a few thousand dollars, including title loans; (2) close the
loophole Ohio had, where lenders registered as brokers to avoid the old rate cap; (3) include protections against
unlicensed online lending, as Ohio's law does, and require licensing for online lenders that lend to state residents; and (4) publish annual loan data so results can be
checked.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** State legislatures pass the law; state financial regulators license lenders, enforce
  it and publish the data. Attorneys general can act against unlicensed online lenders. Ohio's bill
  was co-sponsored by a Republican and a Democrat, so this does not have to be a partisan fight.
- **People and companies with means:** Banks and credit unions can commit to offering small
  installment loans within the new caps so credit doesn't dry up. Responsible lenders and fintechs can
  show legislators their loss rates at these prices. Foundations can fund the borrower research and
  data analysis that gave Ohio's legislators confidence, the way Pew did there.
- **You, this week:** Find out what your state allows. Your state's financial regulator (often called
  the department of financial institutions or banking) lists licensed lenders and takes complaints.
  Before signing any payday or title loan, ask for the total dollar cost, not just the fee, and check
  the lender is licensed in your state. Then tell your state legislator, in two sentences, what a
  payday or title loan cost you.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps the people Pew counts as the 12 million Americans who use payday loans each year, most of
whom use them for rent, utilities and food. Borrowers pay far less per loan. Lenders earn less per
loan; some storefronts close or change their products. The cost to the state is regulation and data
collection, which license fees can cover. Savings start when the law takes effect, though lenders need
months to change products.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Some lenders may leave the state. In Ohio, Pew reports that lower-cost lenders that had avoided the
  state obtained licenses after the law passed, but other states should still pair reform with credit
  union and bank alternatives.
- Lenders may shift to products the law doesn't cover, such as open-ended credit lines or loans made
  through out-of-state partners, so the definitions must be broad and kept up to date.
- A cap that is set too low could leave people with weak credit no legal option, pushing some toward
  unlicensed online lenders. Ohio chose 28% plus a capped fee rather than a flat low cap to keep
  lenders in the market.
- Results from one state don't guarantee the same outcome elsewhere. Published data lets each state
  check its own.
