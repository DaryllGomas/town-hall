# A required, stand-alone personal finance course in high school

## Summary

Many states now require students to take personal finance before graduating. The best evidence says the design matters: a real course with trained teachers and a set curriculum improves young adults' credit scores, while a requirement folded into other classes often never reaches students. States can fund the version that works.

## How it works

- The state requires a semester-long, stand-alone personal finance course (budgeting, credit, debt, saving and investing, insurance) for every graduate.
- The state sets the curriculum, trains teachers and tests what students learned. Georgia's mandate included teacher training before teaching began and an end-of-course assessment.
- Students cannot skip it by testing out or doing a side project, since that is how some mandates reach fewer students.

Evidence from states that did it:

- A 2015 study by researchers at Montana State, the Federal Reserve Board, and the University of Wisconsin-Madison tracked credit records of young adults after mandates in Georgia, Idaho and Texas, compared with similar states without them. Three years in, credit scores were higher by 10.89 points in Georgia, 16.19 in Idaho and 31.71 in Texas, and severe delinquency was lower.
- A 2025 study, as summarized by Education Week, found stand-alone courses improved credit scores through age 34, while embedded approaches showed no measurable effect.
- Education Week also reports an Urban Institute finding that only 39% of students get any personal finance teaching in states with embedded requirements.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** State legislatures and education agencies can require a stand-alone course, fund teacher training and a curriculum, and measure results. About 39 states have passed some requirement (Council for Economic Education, via Education Week), but 13 of them allow the material to be embedded in other courses, so upgrading the design is the next step.
- **People and companies with means:** Banks, employers and foundations can fund teacher training, classroom materials and the evaluation, and offer to guest-teach in under-resourced schools.
- **You, this week:** Ask your local school board or principal whether personal finance is a stand-alone course and who teaches it. If you have a teenager, ask whether it counts toward graduation.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It reaches every student, including those whose families never talked about money. Education Week reports that many state mandates are underfunded and that stand-alone courses cost more to develop. Costs are teacher training, curriculum and a place in the schedule. Effects show up over years, not weeks.

## Trade-offs and risks

A course takes the place of another class or elective. Teachers can be hard to find and legislation is sometimes delayed or weakened before it takes effect. The 2015 study looked at three states, and effect sizes varied widely by state, so results will not match everywhere. Adults who are already out of school are not helped.
