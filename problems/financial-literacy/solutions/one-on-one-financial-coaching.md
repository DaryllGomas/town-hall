# One-on-one financial coaching through employers and community groups

## Summary

Financial coaching pairs a person with a trained coach who works on their own goals, such as paying down debt, building savings or fixing a credit report. A randomized trial of two U.S. programs found people offered coaching saved more, owed less and had higher credit scores than similar people who were not offered it.

## How it works

- A coach meets with the person one on one, usually for a handful of sessions, and builds a plan around what that person wants to fix first.
- The CFPB describes coaching as "customized to meet each person's goals," which separates it from a classroom lesson.
- The CFPB-funded trial, run by the Urban Institute, randomly assigned 945 people to be offered coaching (479) or not (466) at Branches in Miami-Dade County and The Financial Clinic in New York City. At Branches, most clients were municipal employees, so coaching reached people through their workplace.

What the trial measured, compared with the control group:

- Clients paid bills on time more often and made more savings deposits.
- At The Financial Clinic, savings rose by $1,187 (clients earned about $22,110 on average) and credit scores rose 21 points.
- At Branches, total debt fell by $10,644.
- Clients reported more confidence and less financial stress.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Cities, states and federal agencies can place coaches inside places people already go, such as benefits offices, libraries and workforce centers, and fund nonprofit coaching groups.
- **People and companies with means:** Employers can offer coaching as a benefit to hourly and lower-wage staff, and foundations and banks can fund community organizations that provide it.
- **You, this week:** Ask your employer's HR office, a local credit union or a community nonprofit whether they offer free financial coaching or counseling, and book one session with one goal, such as a payoff plan for your smallest debt.

## Who it helps and what it costs

The trial served low- and moderate-income people; the Financial Clinic's clients averaged about $22,110 in income. Results were measured in account balances and credit records, not only surveys. I did not find cost per client in the CFPB materials I read, so cost is unverified.

## Trade-offs and risks

The trial looked at two programs in two cities, and not every measure improved at both sites, so results may vary. Clients attended on average about three sessions, and people with the least time or trust may not sign up. Quality depends on training and on coaches who are not selling products.
