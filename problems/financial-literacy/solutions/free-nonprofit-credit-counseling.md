# Free or low-cost nonprofit credit counseling (NFCC)

## Summary

If debt, bills or credit feel out of control, a certified nonprofit credit counselor will go through your whole situation with you and build a plan. An independent study of one National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) program found clients cut debt and improved credit more than similar people who were not counseled.

## How it works

1. You contact a nonprofit agency, for example one in the NFCC network, by video chat or through the NFCC agency finder.
2. A certified counselor reviews your income, bills and debts and helps you build a budget.
3. If it fits, the counselor can set up a debt management plan with your creditors. The CFPB says to confirm creditors have accepted the plan before sending any payments.

The evidence: Ohio State University researchers compared 6,094 clients of NFCC's Sharpen Your Financial Focus program with 6,005 similar people who were not counseled. Over 18 months, clients cut revolving debt by nearly $6,000 (about $3,600 more than the comparison group) and total debt by almost $9,000. NFCC reports that clients in the bottom quarter of credit scores gained 50 points on average. The study was run with NFCC involvement, so treat the figures as encouraging, not final.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States and the federal government can fund counseling agencies, require lenders and debt collectors to tell people where free counseling exists, and refer people at tax time or benefits offices.
- **People and companies with means:** Employers can offer counseling as a benefit, and banks and credit unions can refer customers who miss payments. Foundations can fund agencies so counselors can reach more people.
- **You, this week:** Go to nfcc.org and request a call or video chat with a counselor, or use the Financial Counseling Association of America or the U.S. Department of Justice approved-agency list that the CFPB points to. Ask about setup and monthly fees up front and get them in writing. Leave any group that refuses to help because you cannot pay.

## Who it helps and what it costs

People with credit card debt, collections or missed payments, and people who want a budget. The CFPB describes counseling as "free or low-cost" advice, but counselors may charge for some services such as debt management plans. Fees vary by agency, so ask first. The NFCC site says you are under no obligation to buy anything.

## Trade-offs and risks

Not every agency is reputable. The CFPB advises checking with your state attorney general, and avoiding groups that charge for educational information or push a debt management plan before reviewing your finances. Counseling does not erase debt, and some people still need other options such as a lawyer.
