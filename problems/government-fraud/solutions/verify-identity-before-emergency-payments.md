# Verify identity before emergency payments, and keep the analytics center

## Summary

Make a shared identity check standard before any large emergency program pays: is the Social Security number valid, does it match the name and birth date, and is the person alive. Pair it with a fast, human appeal for real applicants, and keep the pandemic analytics center as a permanent shared service. In pandemic programs, this check was largely missing.

## How it works

- **What is new:** Today each program builds or skips its own checks. This proposes one standing, pre-built identity check that any emergency program can switch on from day one, instead of relying on self-certification.
- **The evidence it could work:** PRAC sent a random sample of 662,000 identity records from 67.5 million funded applications in three pandemic programs to the Social Security Administration. SSA flagged nearly 24,000 as potentially fraudulent. PRAC's modeling puts the potential identity fraud at about $79 billion, which it says is potential and not confirmed. Its alert argues that verifying identities and flagging anomalies before funds go out would have prevented many such payments.
- **How it would run:** Congress authorizes SSA and Treasury to provide identity verification to emergency programs at launch. Agencies send identity fields, get back match results and flags, and route flagged cases to staff, not to automatic denial.
- **Analytics center:** PRAC's platform, using more than 60 data sources and more than 1 billion records, has supported 1,340 investigations and more than $2.6 billion in potential fraud as of September 30, 2026. PRAC's chair has called for keeping an antifraud analytics center after PRAC ends. This idea makes that center permanent and available to all inspectors general.
- GAO names eligibility verification gaps in emergency programs and self-certification as contributing factors.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Congress writes the authority and funding into emergency-spending laws before the next emergency. SSA and Treasury build a standard verification service. OMB sets a default that emergency programs launch with it.
- **People and companies with means:** Banks, payroll providers and tax preparers can verify identity at application time using the same checks, and fintech and data firms can offer privacy-protecting matching tools to agencies. Philanthropies can fund legal aid groups that help people whose records were wrongly flagged.
- **You, this week:** Check that your own name, birth date and Social Security record match across your tax and benefit accounts, and tell your state representatives you want fast verification with an appeal path for emergency aid.

## Who it helps and what it costs

Taxpayers, honest applicants (who compete for the same limited funds) and program staff. The cost is building and running a verification service and staffing appeals. Treasury's Do Not Pay shows a comparable service can run free to agencies. No cost estimate exists for this specific proposal.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Names and birth dates mismatch for innocent reasons (typos, name changes, recent immigrants, young adults), so a flag must lead to review and a quick appeal, never straight to denial. An emergency program that delays real recipients defeats its purpose.
- PRAC's $79 billion is an extrapolation of potential fraud from a sample, not confirmed losses.
- A central identity service concentrates sensitive data and needs strict access limits and audits.
- Verification stops one kind of fraud (false identities). It does not catch fraud by people with real identities, or error.
