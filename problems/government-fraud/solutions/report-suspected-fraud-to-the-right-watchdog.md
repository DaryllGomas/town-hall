# Report suspected fraud to the right watchdog

## Summary

Anyone can report suspected fraud, waste or abuse of federal money. Oversight.gov, run by the inspectors general council, helps you find the right inspector general office, and GAO's FraudNet takes reports about federal funds and refers them. Reporting gives the people who audit and investigate programs a lead they may not have.

## How it works

- **Find the right office:** Go to Oversight.gov's page "Where to report fraud, waste, abuse, or retaliation" and answer the prompts. It asks whether you have reason to believe there was gross mismanagement or waste of funds, abuse of authority, a substantial danger to public health or safety, or a violation of law, and then directs you to the right agency or entity.
- **GAO FraudNet:** It takes reports of suspected fraud, waste, abuse or mismanagement of federal funds and refers them to the appropriate federal, state or local agencies. The online form is preferred and has three filing choices: standard, confidential (your name is kept confidential but they may contact you) and anonymous (no follow-up possible). You get a control number to add information or ask for status. If a report is referred elsewhere you are told, unless you filed anonymously.
- **If you work for the government and face retaliation:** The Office of Special Counsel handles retaliation complaints from civilian executive branch employees. Oversight.gov explains who is protected: a disclosure made on a reasonable belief of wrongdoing to someone authorized to receive it.
- **Check results afterward:** Oversight.gov also hosts inspector general reports, investigative press releases and open recommendations, so you can see what offices have published.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Inspectors general can keep hotline pages current, confirm receipt, and tell reporters when a tip is referred. Congress can fund hotline staffing and protect whistleblowers.
- **People and companies with means:** Employers, banks and nonprofits that handle public money can set up internal channels that point staff to the right watchdog. Legal aid groups and philanthropies can fund whistleblower counseling.
- **You, this week:** Write down what you saw: the program, who or what office paid or received, dates, and amounts. Do not include things you cannot back up. Use Oversight.gov's reporting page to find the right office, or GAO FraudNet if you are not sure. Leave full Social Security numbers and bank details out of the first report. Offer them only if an investigator asks.

## Who it helps and what it costs

Taxpayers and people relying on programs benefit when investigators get leads. Reporting is free and takes a short form. Results are slow and not guaranteed. Anonymous reports cannot be followed up.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Oversight.gov's page does not say what a report should include, how anonymity works at each office, or how to reach law enforcement. Check the agency's own hotline page for those details.
- Most improper payments are errors, not fraud, so something that looks wrong may have an innocent explanation. Report what you saw and let investigators decide.
- Retaliation protection depends on who you are and where you report. Ask before you disclose if you are a government employee or contractor.
- A report is not a finding. Don't repeat an allegation about a named person in public.
