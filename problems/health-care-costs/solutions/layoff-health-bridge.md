# Layoff health bridge: employers pay three months of coverage and a sign-up helper

## Summary

When a company lays people off, it would pay for each worker's health coverage for three months, either by paying COBRA premiums or by giving the same amount as cash for a Marketplace plan. It would also give every worker an appointment with a free enrollment helper before their last day. The aim is that nobody goes from a paycheck to no insurance in the same week. What's new is making this a standard, public pledge with a fixed price tag, rather than something negotiated case by case.

## How it works

1. **The pledge.** Employers sign a public "layoff health bridge" commitment: any worker laid off gets three months of coverage paid, at the same coverage level they had (single or family).
2. **Two options for the worker.** (a) Stay on the old plan through COBRA with the employer paying the premium, which is best for people in the middle of treatment. (b) Take the same dollar amount and use it toward a Marketplace plan. Losing job-based coverage opens a 60-day Special Enrollment Period, and Marketplace savings are based on expected income for the year, not job status (HealthCare.gov). Some workers will qualify for savings or Medicaid once their income drops.
3. **A helper before the last day.** HR books each worker a session with a certified enrollment assister (free through HealthCare.gov's Find Local Help) or a broker paid by the company. The worker leaves with a decision, not a packet.
4. **A plain one-page notice.** Coverage end date, the 60-day deadline, the COBRA price, an estimated Marketplace price, and where to find a community health center in the meantime.
5. **Public reporting.** Signers publish once a year how many laid-off workers used the bridge and how many had coverage 90 days later.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States could give a tax credit to employers that fund the bridge, or require it for layoffs big enough to need advance notice under the federal WARN Act and state versions of it. Unemployment offices could send every new claimant to a Marketplace application and a local assister.
- **People and companies with means:** Large employers can sign the pledge now and build it into severance. Investors and boards can ask for it in any restructuring plan. Benefits brokers and payroll companies can offer it as a standard add-on. Philanthropists and foundations can set up a pooled fund that pays the bridge for workers at small businesses that close or downsize and can't afford it. That is where the gap is, since COBRA generally applies only to employers with 20 or more workers (Department of Labor).
- **You, this week:** If you're being laid off, ask HR two questions in writing: "Will the company pay any of my COBRA premiums, or give me the equivalent in cash?" and "Can I meet with a benefits or enrollment helper before my last day?" Then fill out a HealthCare.gov application to compare prices before you choose.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps laid-off workers and their families, especially anyone in treatment or on daily medicines. In 2025 the average job-based plan cost $9,325 a year for one person and $26,993 for a family (KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey). Three months of the full premium works out to about $2,330 per worker with single coverage and about $6,750 with family coverage. The employer was already paying most of that while the person worked: on average workers paid only $1,440 (single) and $6,850 (family) a year. The cost falls on the employer, or on a state tax credit or a charitable fund for small businesses. It works from the first layoff after a company signs.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Bigger severance costs could make some employers cut deeper, or give less cash severance in exchange.
- Three months may not be enough in a slow job market. Workers still need to plan for month four.
- Workers who take the cash option might not spend it on coverage. Paying the insurer or Marketplace plan directly avoids that but adds paperwork.
- Small businesses can't easily afford it, which is why a pooled fund or tax credit matters.
- A voluntary pledge only covers companies that sign. Results need public reporting to be believed.
