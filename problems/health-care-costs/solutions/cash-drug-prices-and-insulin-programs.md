# Check the cash price and use $35 insulin programs before you skip a prescription

## Summary

In early 2026, 27% of adults said they hadn't filled a prescription in the past year because of cost (KFF). For many common generics, the cash price at a low-markup pharmacy is lower than people expect. For insulin, every Medicare plan caps the cost at $35 a month, and Lilly sells its insulins for $35 a month to people with commercial insurance or no insurance through December 31, 2026. Checking these takes about 15 minutes.

## How it works

- **Low-markup pharmacies.** Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company lists more than 2,300 medicines, adds a fixed 15% markup and mails them to you (AJMC, May 2026). Your prescriber sends the prescription there and you pay cash.
- **Federal price lookup.** In May 2026 TrumpRx.gov, a federal drug price site, added more than 600 generic medicines. It works as a price comparison site, not a pharmacy, and for some generics it sends users to Cost Plus (AJMC). HealthCare.gov points people without drug coverage to it.
- **Insulin with Medicare.** Medicare caps each covered insulin at $35 for a one-month supply, with no deductible. That includes people on Extra Help (Medicare.gov).
- **Insulin without Medicare.** Lilly's Insulin Value Program prices all Lilly insulins at $35 a month at retail pharmacies, whether you have commercial insurance or none. You download a savings card from insulins.lilly.com and show it at the pharmacy. You must be 18 or older, live in the U.S. and not be enrolled in a government health program. The card ends December 31, 2026, and Lilly can change it. The American Diabetes Association keeps a list of other makers' programs, including Sanofi's $35-a-month Insulins Valyou program for uninsured people and Novo Nordisk's patient assistance program. Check each maker's current terms.
- **Free medicine for low incomes.** Drug makers run patient assistance programs that give medicine free to people who qualify by income, for example Lilly Cares, Novo Nordisk's program and Sanofi Patient Connection (American Diabetes Association).

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Congress could extend a $35 monthly insulin cap to private insurance and to uninsured people, matching the Medicare rule. States can do the same for plans they regulate. Medicaid programs and state employee plans can test low-markup pharmacy pricing for common generics.
- **People and companies with means:** Drug makers can extend $35 insulin cards past their end dates, publish them in plain language and let pharmacists apply them automatically for cash patients. Employers that pay for their own health plans can add a low-markup mail pharmacy for generics and pass the savings to workers. Hospital systems can check the cash price of every discharge prescription before a patient leaves. Philanthropists can pay for pharmacists at free clinics and health centers who help patients sign up for assistance programs.
- **You, this week:**
  1. Write down each medicine, its strength and how many pills a month.
  2. Look it up at costplusdrugs.com and at TrumpRx.gov. Call your usual pharmacy and ask for its cash price too. Choose the cheapest one.
  3. If you use Lilly insulin and don't have Medicare or Medicaid, download the $35 card at insulins.lilly.com before your next refill. For other brands, check the American Diabetes Association list at diabetes.org.
  4. If you can't afford even those prices, ask your doctor's office or a community health center to help you apply for the maker's patient assistance program.
  5. Don't cut pills or skip doses without asking your prescriber. Ask whether a cheaper generic or a different drug would work instead.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps people without drug coverage, people whose plan has a high deductible and people who use insulin. The cost to the patient is the cash price; at Cost Plus that is the company's cost plus a fixed 15% markup. The savings start with the next refill. Drug makers pay for the insulin discounts and assistance programs.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Cash purchases outside your insurance may not count toward your deductible. Lilly's cash card can't be reimbursed from insurance or an HSA or FSA.
- If you have insurance, your plan's copay may be lower than the cash price for some drugs, so compare both.
- Mail-order delivery takes days, so don't wait until your last dose.
- Manufacturer programs are voluntary and can change or end. Lilly's current card runs only through December 31, 2026.
- Low-markup pharmacies mostly help with generics. New brand-name drugs and specialty drugs can still be expensive.
