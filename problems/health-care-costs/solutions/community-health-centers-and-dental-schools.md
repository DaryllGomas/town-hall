# Community health centers and dental schools: care priced by your income

## Summary

Federally funded community health centers see patients with or without insurance, and what you pay depends on your income. Many also offer dental and mental health care. Dental schools run supervised student clinics that are usually cheaper than private offices. Both already exist in most of the country, and many people who skip care because of cost don't know about them.

## How it works

- **Health centers.** HealthCare.gov describes them as a low-cost option for people who can't afford a plan and don't qualify for Medicaid or CHIP: "How much you pay depends on your income." They offer primary care, prenatal care, vaccinations and referrals, including for mental health and substance use care.
- **Scale.** Health centers served 32.7 million patients in 2025, and 17% of them had no insurance. Of more than 144 million visits, 12% were dental and 15% were mental health or substance use care. (KFF)
- **Dental schools.** The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR) says dental school clinics let students treat patients under licensed dentists' supervision, often at lower cost. Dental hygiene schools may also offer low-cost cleanings. NIDCR also points to Medicaid and CHIP (all states cover children's dental care; adult dental coverage varies by state), state and local health departments, 2-1-1 and dental clinical trials.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** KFF reports that federal health center funding expires in December 2026 and that the regular annual amount hasn't been set yet. Congress can fund health centers for several years at a time so they can hire staff and plan. States can add adult dental benefits to Medicaid and pay health center dental teams enough to keep them. Cities can help with space for school-based and mobile clinics.
- **People and companies with means:** Hospital systems can send uninsured patients leaving the emergency room to a nearby health center for follow-up care, and pay for a staffer who books the appointment before the patient goes home. Employers with hourly workers can partner with a local center for on-site or after-hours clinics. Philanthropists and foundations can pay for dental chairs, mobile dental vans and dental residents at centers. Dental schools' donors can fund free-care days for uninsured patients.
- **You, this week:**
  1. Go to findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov (the federal health center finder that HealthCare.gov links to), type your address and pick the nearest center.
  2. Call and ask: "Do you have a sliding fee scale? What do I need to bring to show my income?" Bring a recent pay stub, an unemployment letter or a tax return.
  3. For dental work, search "dental school clinic" plus your state, or call 2-1-1 and ask about free or reduced-cost dental care.
  4. Ask the center whether it has an on-site or partner pharmacy with discounted prices.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps uninsured and underinsured people, people who just lost a job, and anyone with a high deductible. Health centers took in $55.4 billion in 2025. Patient care payments (Medicaid, private insurance, Medicare and self-pay) were 76% of that, federal health center grants were 10%, and other grants were 14% (KFF). For a patient it costs a reduced fee set by income, and it works as soon as you get an appointment. Dental school care can cost less than a private office, but expect longer appointments and more visits while students learn under supervision.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Waits for appointments can be long, and not every center offers dental care.
- Sliding fees lower the bill but may not make it zero. Specialist care and hospital stays outside the center are billed separately.
- If health center funding is cut or delayed, centers may cut hours or services. KFF expects more uninsured patients as Marketplace subsidies shrink and Medicaid rules change.
- Dental school clinics follow the school calendar and may not take urgent cases.
