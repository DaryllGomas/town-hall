# Employer emergency savings accounts with a seed and a match

## Summary

Employers can give workers a separate, easy-to-reach emergency savings account funded straight from the
paycheck, ideally with a starting deposit and a match. Federal law now allows this inside a retirement
plan (a "pension-linked emergency savings account", or PLESA), and large employers already run
stand-alone versions. Early results show people use the money for emergencies instead of raiding their
401(k).

## How it works

Two models exist today:

- **PLESA (inside the 401(k) plan).** Since plan years beginning after December 31, 2023, employers can
  add an emergency savings account to their retirement plan for workers who are not highly compensated.
  Workers can be auto-enrolled at up to 3% of pay, with notice and the right to opt out. Their
  contributions are capped at $2,500 (indexed to inflation). Workers can withdraw at least once a month
  with no proof of emergency, and no fees can be charged just for the first four withdrawals in a plan
  year. Any employer match on PLESA contributions goes to the retirement side.
- **Stand-alone "sidecar" accounts.** An employer partners with a bank or fintech so a worker can direct
  a slice of each paycheck into a savings account, often with sign-up bonuses or matches paid by the
  employer.

What makes these work for people with no cushion: auto-enrollment, a seed deposit so the account isn't
empty on day one, a match or bonus for keeping a balance, and instant, fee-free withdrawals.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Congress could let employers put matches directly into the PLESA (today matches go to
  the retirement side) and give small businesses a tax credit for seeding accounts. States could add an
  emergency savings pocket to any state-run savings program for workers without a workplace plan.
- **People and companies with means:** Every large employer can turn this on. The ambitious version:
  seed every hourly worker's account with $500 on day one and match the next $500. Foundations can follow The BlackRock Foundation, which
  committed $50 million to this work, by paying for seed deposits at small employers that can't afford
  them.
- **You, this week:** Ask HR or your benefits portal whether your employer offers an emergency savings
  account or PLESA. If it does, sign up, even at $10 a paycheck. If not, set up a split direct deposit
  that sends a fixed small amount to a separate savings account you don't see every day.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps workers with steady jobs who have no buffer, and it protects their retirement savings.
BlackRock reports that its Emergency Savings Initiative, run with the nonprofit Commonwealth since 2019,
expanded access to more than 22 million workers and helped generate nearly $8 billion in emergency
savings. More than 28% of workers who opened an account made a withdrawal (median $640), which the
initiative says helped preserve up to $38 million in retirement assets. At Starbucks, emergency savers'
401(k) contribution rates were almost triple those of non-savers, and one in five people not saving
for retirement started after opening an emergency account. Vanguard reports 16% lower hardship
withdrawal rates in plans offering its Cash Plus Account. Costs to employers are mainly setup, any seed
or match money, and administration.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Most results come from the programs' own sponsors and compare savers with non-savers, so they show
  association, not proof that the accounts caused the better outcomes.
- It only helps people with an employer that offers it; gig and independent workers are left out.
- Workers whose pay barely covers bills may not be able to save at all. Without a seed or match, the
  account may stay near zero for the people who most need it.
- Auto-enrolling someone living paycheck to paycheck can tip them into an overdraft if the deduction
  isn't clearly explained.
