# A "first $1,000" cushion fund, seeded by people and companies with means

## Summary

Most advice tells people with no savings to start saving. That's hard when every dollar is spoken for.
This idea flips the order: philanthropists and companies put the first $1,000 into a worker's own
emergency account, and the worker keeps it topped up with matched small deposits. The cushion exists on
day one, so the next car repair or late paycheck doesn't become a debt spiral or a 401(k) cash-out.

## How it works

1. **A regional fund is set up** by donors, a community foundation and local employers, with a bank or
   credit union holding the accounts.
2. **Who qualifies:** working adults below a set income line (for example, household income under
   $50,000), enrolled through their employer, a credit union, a community college or a 211 agency so it
   reaches gig and hourly workers too.
3. **The seed:** each person gets $1,000 in an FDIC- or NCUA-insured savings account in their own name.
   It's theirs to use for any emergency, no proof required.
4. **The refill match:** when they use it, the fund matches deposits (say $1 for every $1, up to $500
   a year) to rebuild it. Deposits can come from split direct deposit or tax refunds.
5. **The measure:** an independent evaluator compares participants with a waitlist group on late bills,
   overdraft and payday-loan use, 401(k) hardship withdrawals, evictions and stress, and publishes the
   results.

**What's new:** existing programs mostly help people who can already save something, or pay out only
after a crisis hits. This fund pays first and rewards keeping it, and it is portable across jobs, so a
layoff doesn't take it away.

**Why it could work:** the gap is often small money. In October 2025, 4 in 10 adults earning under
$50,000 said they could not cover a $100 emergency from savings, and 59% of all adults had a major
unexpected expense in the prior year, most often a vehicle repair. Workplace programs show people use
emergency accounts as intended: in BlackRock's initiative, more than 28% of account holders made a
withdrawal (median $640), and the initiative says this helped preserve up to $38 million in retirement
assets. One-time emergency cash has reduced homelessness in rigorous studies (see
[fast-one-time-emergency-cash.md](fast-one-time-emergency-cash.md)).

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Cities or states could add public matching money, and Congress could make donor-funded
  seed deposits tax-free to the recipient and not count them against benefit asset limits, so the
  cushion doesn't cost anyone their SNAP or Medicaid. (Those rules vary by program and would need
  checking.)
- **People and companies with means:** This is the ask. $10 million seeds 10,000 workers. A billionaire,
  a family foundation or a coalition of a region's largest employers could fund a whole metro area. The
  BlackRock Foundation's $50 million commitment to workplace emergency savings shows the scale is
  realistic. Employers can enroll their own low-wage staff and add the refill match. Banks and credit
  unions can hold the accounts with no fees and no minimums.
- **You, this week:** Start your own first $100 in a separate account. SaverLife, a philanthropy-funded
  nonprofit, offers cash rewards for building savings habits and reports that 61% of members reach $500
  in savings within six months of joining.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps low- and moderate-income workers who have no cushion and are one bill away from borrowing.
The direct cost is $1,000 per person plus up to $500 a year in matches and a modest cost for running
it; for 10,000 people that is roughly $10 million to start and up to $5 million a year in matches.
Donors and employers pay; the worker pays nothing. It works on day one.

## Trade-offs and risks

- This hasn't been tested at this design and scale; it needs a pilot with a real evaluation before it
  grows.
- $1,000 won't cover a layoff. It covers the most common shocks, not every one.
- Some money will go to people who would have managed anyway. Income targeting reduces but doesn't
  remove this.
- Savings can count against asset limits for some public benefits; the fund must check and design
  around this, or people could be hurt by the help.
- Donor money can dry up. Pairing it with employer matches and public dollars makes it last.
