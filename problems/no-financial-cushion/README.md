# Living paycheck to paycheck

No cushion: one late paycheck, car repair, layoff or medical bill away from everything falling over.
The goal here is practical: help people get through a stretch of instability and out the other side
to stability.

## The issue

About 1 in 3 adults can't cover a $400 surprise with cash, and for 4 in 10 adults earning under
$50,000, even $100 is out of reach from savings. Big unexpected costs like car repairs, home repairs
and medical bills hit most adults in a given year, so without a cushion one of them turns into missed
bills, late rent or high-cost debt. When pay is delayed, hours are cut or a job ends, more people now
turn to retirement savings, where income tax, a possible 10% additional tax and 20% withholding up front
make it an expensive emergency fund. Young adults and renters show the strain most: nearly half of
adults 18 to 29 needed outside help with an expense, and nearly a quarter of renters fell behind on
rent in the past year.

## The solution

**Every working household should have a small cushion to fall back on, and fast help when that isn't enough, before a bill becomes a debt or an eviction.**

1. **Cities, counties and states:** fund year-round emergency assistance pools that pay landlords and
   utilities within days, with phone or text applications, and have courts refer tenants at every
   eviction filing. ([evidence](solutions/fast-one-time-emergency-cash.md))
2. **Employers:** offer emergency savings accounts with auto-enrollment, a seed deposit, a match and
   fee-free withdrawals. ([evidence](solutions/employer-emergency-savings-accounts.md))
3. **Employers and retirement plan providers:** switch on the $1,000 emergency withdrawal and show the
   real after-tax cost of a full cash-out before anyone takes one.
   ([evidence](solutions/before-you-cash-out-a-401k.md))
4. **Philanthropy and regional employers:** pilot a "first $1,000" fund that seeds low-income workers'
   own savings accounts and matches refills, with an independent evaluation.
   ([evidence](solutions/first-1000-cushion-fund.md))
5. **Congress:** let employer matches go straight into workplace emergency savings accounts and give
   small businesses a tax credit for seeding them.
   ([evidence](solutions/employer-emergency-savings-accounts.md))

## What we know

- **About 1 in 3 adults can't cover a $400 surprise with cash.** In the Federal Reserve's survey of
  October 2025, 63% of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense using cash,
  savings or a credit card paid off at the next statement. That share was unchanged from the previous
  three years and down from 68% in 2021.
  ([Federal Reserve, 2025 fact sheet](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/other20260513a1.pdf))
- **For lower-income adults the gap is much smaller money.** Among adults with income under $50,000,
  4 in 10 said they could not cover even a $100 emergency expense with only their savings.
  ([Federal Reserve, 2025 fact sheet](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/other20260513a1.pdf))
- **Emergencies are common, not rare.** 59% of adults had at least one major unexpected expense in the
  prior 12 months. The most common were a major vehicle repair or replacement (30% of adults), a major
  house or appliance repair (22%) and unexpected major medical expenses (21%).
  ([Federal Reserve, 2025 fact sheet](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/other20260513a1.pdf))
- **Bills and rent are slipping.** 16% of adults did not pay all of their bills in the prior month, and
  23% of renters had been behind on rent at some point in the past year, up 6 percentage points since
  2021. 47% of adults aged 18 to 29 got help from someone outside their household to pay an expense.
  ([Federal Reserve, 2025 fact sheet](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/other20260513a1.pdf))
- **Retirement money is becoming the emergency fund.** About 6% of eligible Vanguard plan participants
  took a hardship withdrawal in 2025, up from 5% in 2024. For participants earning under $100,000,
  about 7 in 10 withdrawals went to avoiding eviction or foreclosure or to medical expenses. 45% of
  participants had less than $2,000 in emergency savings.
  ([Vanguard, March 2026](https://workplace.vanguard.com/insights-and-research/perspective/how-america-uses-hardship-withdrawals.html))
- **Cashing out costs more than it looks.** Hardship distributions are taxed as income, may carry a 10%
  additional tax, and can't be repaid to the plan. A cash-out from a workplace plan has 20% withheld up
  front. ([IRS, hardship distributions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-hardship-distributions),
  [IRS, rollovers](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/rollovers-of-retirement-plan-and-ira-distributions))
- **More savers now plan to tap retirement in an emergency.** Commonwealth, a nonprofit, reports that the
  share of savers who say they would draw on retirement savings in an emergency rose from 33% in 2020
  to 57%. ([Commonwealth](https://buildcommonwealth.org/rpa))

## Open questions

- Which employer emergency savings designs (seed money, matches, auto-enrollment) reach low-wage and
  hourly workers, not just the ones who already save?
- How much does a one-time cash grant at the moment of crisis change where people are a year later,
  beyond housing?
- What happens to people's finances after hours are cut, and what helps them bridge it?
- How can someone laid off keep their 401(k) intact while still paying rent: which options, in what
  order?
- What would it cost to give every worker a first $1,000 cushion, and who would fund it?
