# Weatherize low-income homes so bills stay lower for good

## Summary

Bill help pays this month's bill; weatherization makes every future bill smaller. The Department of
Energy's Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP) pays local agencies to seal, insulate and repair the
homes of low-income households for free. National evaluations found the energy savings alone exceed
the cost, and the health and safety benefits make it a much better deal.

## How it works

- DOE gives formula grants to all 50 states, D.C., tribes and five territories, which contract with
  roughly 700 local agencies.
- A household applies to its local agency. Households at or below 200% of the federal poverty
  guidelines, or receiving Supplemental Security Income, are eligible; states may instead use LIHEAP's
  limit of 60% of state median income. Priority goes to older people, people with disabilities,
  families with children and households with high energy use or burden. Renters can apply with their
  landlord's permission.
- An auditor reviews the energy bills, runs a blower-door test to measure air leaks and checks
  equipment for health and safety problems. The agency then installs the most cost-effective package
  of measures, and an inspector checks the work.
- **Results:** DOE says weatherized households save on average $372 or more every year, and about
  32,000 homes a year are weatherized with DOE funds. Oak Ridge National Laboratory's evaluations found
  energy savings exceed costs by a factor of 1.4, rising to about 4 when health and safety benefits are
  counted.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Congress funds WAP each year; DOE announced over $400 million for the program in July
  2025. Funding enough to clear waiting lists, and letting states combine it with LIHEAP's
  weatherization share and utility efficiency programs, would reach more homes. State energy offices
  can prioritize rural homes and homes heated with fuel oil, whose price rose 74.2% in the year to
  September 2026.
- **People and companies with means:** In 2018 the network brought in $679 million from non-DOE
  sources, $3.48 for every DOE dollar. Utilities can fund more jobs through their efficiency programs;
  philanthropy can pay for the roof and wiring repairs that otherwise keep a home from qualifying;
  landlords can agree to weatherization and keep rents steady afterward.
- **You, this week:** Find your state weatherization office through DOE's "how to apply" page (linked
  below) or USA.gov, and ask the local agency to add you to the list. Bring proof of the last year's
  income. If you rent, ask your landlord to sign the permission form. If you're applying for LIHEAP,
  ask the same office about weatherization.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps low-income owners and renters, especially in older, drafty homes, and lowers bills for years
  after a single visit.
- In program year 2008, the average cost to weatherize a home was $4,695, with DOE paying 48%. DOE
  spent $236 million that year, and total spending on the 97,965 homes weatherized, including other
  funding, was $481 million.
- Paid mostly by federal funds plus state, utility and private money. Savings start the first month
  after the work, but waiting lists can be long.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Waiting lists can take months or longer, so it won't help this winter's first bill.
- Homes needing major repairs, such as a leaking roof, may be deferred because the program can't fix
  them.
- Savings depend on the home and the fuel; the evaluations' figures are averages from earlier years.
- Renters may face higher rents after upgrades unless landlords agree not to raise them.
