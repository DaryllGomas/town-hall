# Cap low-income utility bills at a share of income

## Summary

A percentage-of-income payment plan (PIPP) bills a low-income household a fixed share of its income
for electricity or gas instead of the full metered bill, and forgives old debt bit by bit for each
on-time payment. Ohio has run one, PIPP Plus, for years; Illinois regulators ordered one for Ameren
Illinois customers starting by June 2026. It turns a bill that swings with fuel prices into one a
family can plan around.

## How it works

- **Ohio PIPP Plus:** Customers of state-regulated electric and gas utilities with household income at
  or below 175% of the federal poverty level pay 5% of monthly income for electric and/or gas service,
  or 10% in all-electric homes, with a $10 minimum. If they pay in full and on time, they don't owe the
  difference between the real bill and the PIPP amount, and each on-time payment forgives 1/24th of any
  old balance. New enrollees pay no security deposit and no late fees. They must also apply for energy
  assistance and weatherization they qualify for, and reverify income every year.
- **Illinois:** In August 2025 the Illinois Commerce Commission approved a plan for Ameren Illinois
  customers with incomes up to 300% of the federal poverty level to pay no more than 3% of monthly
  income for electricity, available by June 1, 2026. The commission rejected the utility's proposed
  4.5% cap (9% for electric heat). Enrolled customers' bills don't rise when rates or usage rise.
- **Enrollment:** In Ohio, customers apply at their local community action agency with photo ID, a
  list of household members, recent utility bills and proof of income.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** State utility commissions can order PIPPs in rate cases, as Illinois did, or
  legislatures can create one statewide, as Ohio has. They should link enrollment to LIHEAP and
  weatherization so one application covers all three, and require utilities to report enrollment,
  shutoffs and cost every year.
- **People and companies with means:** Utilities can propose a PIPP themselves instead of waiting for
  an order, and enroll customers automatically when they get LIHEAP. Foundations can fund community
  agencies to help people enroll and to evaluate results. Large energy users can support funding the
  program in rate cases.
- **You, this week:** Call your utility and ask: "Do you have a percentage-of-income plan, a low-income
  discount rate or an arrearage forgiveness program?" In Ohio, book an appointment at your community
  action agency for PIPP Plus. In Ameren Illinois territory, ask about the new income-based rate. Have
  your last bill and 30 days of income proof ready.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps low-income households with high bills compared with income, especially those with old debt or
  electric heat.
- The gap between capped payments and actual bills has to be covered, by other customers' rates or by
  public funds, depending on how the state designs it. The sources here don't give program costs; NCLC
  notes PIPPs may take somewhat more administration than straight discount rates, because each
  customer needs a separate billing arrangement.
- Works from the first bill after enrollment.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Other customers may pay more to cover the gap; regulators need to publish the cost.
- A capped bill weakens the price signal to save energy, so plans should be paired with weatherization
  (Ohio requires enrollees to apply for it).
- Income checks every year add paperwork, and households that miss a payment can lose the benefit.
- Only works where a state or utility sets one up; most households don't have access yet.
