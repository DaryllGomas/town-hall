# An employer commute-cost package: fewer trips, shared rides and pre-tax transit

## Summary

Most workers drive alone to work, so when gasoline prices jump, the commute is where the pain lands.
Employers control the biggest lever: how many days a week people have to make that drive. A
"commute-cost package" bundles fewer trips (remote or four-day schedules where the job allows), shared
rides (employer vanpools) and pre-tax transit and vanpool benefits, switched on whenever fuel prices
spike.

## How it works

- **The problem it targets:** In 2022, 68.7% of U.S. workers drove alone to work. Gasoline prices rose
  34.2% from September 2025 to September 2026, and low-income households already spend 17.8% of income
  on home energy and car fuel combined, with rural and suburban workers relying on cars for long trips.
- **Fewer trips:** For jobs that can be done from home part of the time, offer one or two remote days a
  week. For on-site jobs, offer an optional four-day, 10-hour schedule: one less round trip a week.
- **Shared rides:** The employer organizes vanpools or carpool matching for workers on the same route,
  especially at rural plants, hospitals and warehouses where transit doesn't reach.
- **Pre-tax benefits:** Federal tax law lets employers offer, through a salary reduction agreement,
  up to $340 a month in 2026 tax-free for transit passes and commuter vanpools (and $340 for qualified
  parking). The tax break for bicycle commuting ended after 2025. Employers can add their
  own contribution on top.
- **The trigger:** Write the package into policy with a trigger, for example when the regional gas price
  rises a set percentage above its level a year earlier, so it starts automatically instead of after
  months of debate.
- **What's new:** Most employers offer one piece, if any. The idea is a standing package, aimed at the
  workers with the longest drives and lowest pay, that turns on when prices spike.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Federal and state agencies can adopt the package for their own workforce. State
  departments of transportation and transit agencies can fund vanpool start-up costs in rural areas.
  States can offer employers technical help or small tax credits for running vanpools.
- **People and companies with means:** Employers can adopt the package now at low cost; remote days and
  schedule changes cost little, and pre-tax benefits are set up through a simple salary reduction
  agreement and are excluded from wages up to the monthly limit. Business
  groups and chambers can publish a model policy. Philanthropy can fund vans for rural employers
  where no vanpool operator exists.
- **You, this week:** Ask HR whether your employer offers pre-tax transit or vanpool benefits, a remote
  day or a compressed schedule, and propose a trial for your team. Look for coworkers on your route to
  share rides, and combine errands into fewer trips.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps commuters with long drives, especially lower-paid on-site workers in rural and suburban areas.
- Costs: little for schedule changes; vanpool vans, insurance and coordination for shared rides; the
  employer's share of any transit subsidy. Pre-tax set-asides cost the employer almost nothing.
- Paid by employers, with possible state or transit agency help for rural vanpools. Remote days and
  schedule changes can start within weeks.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Many jobs can't be done remotely, and 10-hour days are hard for workers with child care or second
  jobs; the options must be voluntary.
- Office-heavy remote policies can help higher-paid workers most unless the package targets
  on-site staff too.
- Vanpools need enough riders on a route and someone to manage them.
- Not yet tested as a single package; a pilot at a few employers should measure fuel savings, turnover
  and absences.
