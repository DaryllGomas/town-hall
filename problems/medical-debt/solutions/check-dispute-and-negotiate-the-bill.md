# Get the itemized bill, dispute what's wrong, and negotiate the rest before paying

## Summary

Medical bills and collection notices often include charges that can't legally be collected: services
never received, amounts insurance already paid, surprise bills banned by federal law, or debts that
should have been covered by financial assistance. Federal rules give people the right to see the
details, dispute the debt in writing and negotiate. Doing this before paying anything can shrink or
erase the amount owed.

## How it works

1. **Ask for an itemized bill.** The CFPB advises asking the provider or the collector for a detailed
   bill (sometimes called a "superbill") showing each procedure code, what insurance paid and what you
   owe. Compare it with your insurer's explanation of benefits.
2. **Look for charges that can't be collected** (CFPB list): services you didn't get, a more expensive
   service than the one you got, bills already paid by you or your insurer, bills that charity care
   should have covered, and surprise out-of-network bills barred by the No Surprises Act (emergency
   care, some care at in-network facilities, air ambulances).
3. **If you were uninsured or paid yourself**, providers generally must give a good faith estimate when
   you schedule care ahead or ask. If the bill is at least $400 more than the estimate, you may be able
   to dispute it through the federal process at cms.gov/medical-bill-rights.
4. **If a collector contacts you,** it must give you the creditor's name, the amount and how to dispute,
   at first contact or within five days. Some rights depend on writing back within 30 days. Use the
   CFPB's free sample letters to dispute the debt or ask for proof, send them quickly and keep copies.
   You can also tell a collector in writing to stop contacting you.
5. **Negotiate what's left.** Ask the provider first, then the collector, for a lower amount or an
   interest-free payment plan. The CFPB notes collectors may not tell you charges can be negotiated.
   Avoid putting medical bills on a credit card or loan.
6. **Check your state's rules.** In Colorado, for example, interest on medical debt is capped at 3% a
   year, a collector must pause collection on written request until it sends an itemized statement, and
   collection must stop while an insurance appeal is pending (SB23-093). Many states also limit
   lawsuits over old debts; a legal aid office can tell you your state's time limit.
7. **If something is wrong,** file a complaint at consumerfinance.gov/complaint and contact legal aid.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States can copy Colorado's itemization-before-collection and appeal-pause rules and
  its 3% interest cap. Federal and state agencies can require every medical bill and collection letter
  to state, in plain words, the right to an itemized bill and to financial assistance. State attorneys
  general can take and publish complaints about medical billing.
- **People and companies with means:** Fund free medical-billing advocates at legal aid offices and
  community health centers. Employers and unions can offer bill review as a benefit.
  Hospitals and physician groups can send an itemized bill automatically with every
  balance over a set amount, and stop interest on patient balances.
- **You, this week:** Pick your largest medical bill. Call or write: "Please send me a fully itemized
  bill with procedure codes, and put the account on hold until I've reviewed it." If a collector has it,
  send the CFPB sample letter asking for proof of the debt and the itemized charges. Pull your insurer's
  explanation of benefits for the same date and mark every charge that doesn't match. Don't pay or agree
  to a payment plan by phone until you have the itemized bill.

## Who it helps and what it costs

Anyone with a medical bill or a medical account in collections, insured or not. For the person, it
costs postage and a few hours. Results range from fixing a single error to removing a whole bill, and
usually take weeks. Providers bear the cost of correcting bills; collectors bear the cost of proving
debts.

## Trade-offs and risks

- It takes time and persistence, which is hard for someone who is sick. Free help from legal aid or
  a patient advocate makes a big difference.
- Ignoring a collector doesn't make it go away; the CFPB says avoiding contact is unlikely to stop it.
  If you're sued, respond by the deadline or you may lose by default.
- Protections vary widely by state, and most state rules cover only hospitals.
- These steps lower or fix bills but don't help with debt that is correct and still unaffordable; for
  that, apply for financial assistance (see the solution on hospital financial assistance in this folder).
