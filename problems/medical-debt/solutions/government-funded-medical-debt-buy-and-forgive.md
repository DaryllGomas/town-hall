# State and local governments buy and cancel residents' medical debt for pennies on the dollar

## Summary

Old medical debt is bought and sold among collectors for a small fraction of its face value. States,
counties and cities now use that market in reverse: they pay the nonprofit Undue Medical Debt to buy
residents' qualifying debt from hospitals and collectors and cancel it. Illinois says it erased more than
$1.4 billion for almost 700,000 residents with under $10 million in state funds. Residents don't apply;
they get a letter saying the debt is gone.

## How it works

1. A state, county or city sets aside money. Orange County, Florida approved $4.5 million in federal
   American Rescue Plan funds in 2024; Cook County, Illinois also started with those funds and is moving
   to its own budget; the State of Illinois used state dollars.
2. It contracts with Undue Medical Debt, which buys bundles of medical debt from hospitals, health
   systems and debt buyers in that area.
3. Debt is cancelled for residents at or under 400% of the federal poverty level, or whose medical
   debt equals 5% or more of their income. These are the rules used in Illinois, New Jersey and
   Massachusetts.
4. People receive a letter after the debt is cancelled. In New Jersey, Illinois and Orange County
   there's no application, and Orange County notes cancelled debt isn't taxed as income.

Examples reported in 2026: Illinois passed $1.4 billion in October, two years in. New Jersey's seventh
round in January erased about $16 million for about 14,000 residents. In Massachusetts, the Atrius
Health Equity Foundation, Undue Medical Debt and the Massachusetts Health & Hospital Association erased
just over $42 million for about 27,000 residents in a round led by a health foundation.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** A governor, legislature, county board or city council appropriates funds and signs a
  contract with a debt-relief nonprofit. To get lasting value, pair relief with rules that stop new debt
  from forming: automatic charity-care screening, limits on interest, liens and garnishment, and
  credit-reporting limits (see the other solutions in this folder).
- **People and companies with means:** Philanthropists can fund a buy-and-forgive round for a whole
  region, as the Atrius Health Equity Foundation did in Massachusetts; at Illinois's ratio, under $10
  million cleared $1.4 billion. Hospitals and health systems can
  sell or donate their unpaid patient accounts to a relief nonprofit instead of to a debt buyer, or cancel
  balances for low-income patients directly. Employers and foundations can match public funds so
  counties without budgets still take part.
- **You, this week:** Ask your state legislator, county commissioner or city council member, by email or
  at a public meeting, whether your area has a medical debt relief program and, if not, to fund one
  modeled on Illinois. If you've had a medical bill go to collections, watch your mail for a relief
  letter, and check your state treasury or health department site for any program. You don't need to
  pay anyone to be included: legitimate programs don't charge.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps lower- and middle-income people whose medical debt has been sold or is held by participating
hospitals and collectors. In Illinois, under $10 million in state money relieved $1.4 billion, so the
public cost per dollar of debt cleared was well under one cent. Relief arrives in waves over months to
years. Taxpayers or donors pay; hospitals and debt buyers receive the sale price for accounts they have
largely written off.

## Trade-offs and risks

- **Limited effect on health and finances.** A randomized study of $169 million in relief for 83,401
  people (Kluender, Mahoney, Wong and Yin; Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025) found modest credit
  improvements where the debt had been reported, a small drop in payments on other medical bills, and
  no measurable effect on mental or physical health or financial wellness. Relief should be paired with
  prevention.
- **Only some debt is reachable.** Programs can only buy debt that hospitals or collectors agree to
  sell; debt on credit cards, medical credit cards or still owed directly to some providers isn't
  covered.
- **It doesn't stop the next bill.** Without changes upstream, new debt keeps forming.
- **Some bills stay.** Orange County warns that other bills from the same episode of care may remain
  unpaid if they weren't part of a purchased bundle.
