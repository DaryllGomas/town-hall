# Screen every patient for financial assistance automatically, before the first bill goes out

## Summary

Most people who qualify for hospital charity care never get it, because they have to find out it exists
and apply while sick or behind on bills. Flip the default: before any patient balance is billed, the
provider checks eligibility automatically using information it's already allowed to use, applies the
discount, and the patient gets a bill they can actually pay, or none at all. Debt that never forms
doesn't need to be forgiven later.

## How it works

1. **At registration or discharge,** the provider runs a screen for every self-pay balance: enrollment in
   Medicaid, SNAP or other means-tested programs, prior assistance approvals, and income estimates. A
   soft credit inquiry, which doesn't affect credit scores, is one tool already used this way: Undue
   Medical Debt used one to decide eligibility for relief in Massachusetts.
2. **If the screen shows the person likely qualifies,** the provider applies free or discounted care
   automatically. Federal 501(r) regulations already let nonprofit hospitals decide eligibility from
   information other than what the patient supplies, and from earlier approvals, as long as the policy
   says so ("presumptive eligibility").
3. **If the screen is inconclusive,** the first bill shows the discount the patient might get, in plain
   words at the top, with a one-page application.
4. **No account goes to collections, credit reporting or a debt buyer** until screening is done and
   documented.
5. **Results are public.** Each hospital reports yearly how many patients were screened, approved and
   sent to collections, so the state and the public can see the gap shrink.

**What's new:** 501(r) allows presumptive eligibility but doesn't require it, and per a 2026 Commonwealth
Fund review only 8 states require hospitals to screen patients for financial assistance or public
coverage before sending accounts to collections. This proposal makes screening universal, puts it
**before the first bill** rather than before collections, extends it to physician groups, ambulances
and other providers that bill separately, and adds public reporting.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** State legislatures can pass a "screen before you bill" law covering all licensed
  providers, with reporting and a patient right to sue if it's skipped (the Commonwealth Fund review
  notes only a handful of states, such as Delaware and New Mexico, give patients that right). State
  Medicaid and SNAP agencies can offer providers a secure yes/no eligibility check. The IRS could make
  presumptive screening a standard part of 501(r) compliance reviews.
- **People and companies with means:** Health systems can adopt this voluntarily now; the rules already
  allow it, and it may cut the cost of chasing bills that later get written off. Foundations can pay for
  a statewide pilot and an independent evaluation at a few hospitals, comparing debt and collections
  before and after. Software and billing companies can build the screen into standard billing systems
  and offer it at low cost to rural and safety-net hospitals.
- **You, this week:** If you're getting care, ask at registration or discharge: "Can you screen me for
  financial assistance before you bill me?" If you already have a bill, apply now (see the hospital
  financial assistance solution in this folder). Then write or call your state legislators and ask them
  to require automatic screening before billing.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps uninsured and underinsured patients with low or moderate incomes, especially people who are
too sick, overwhelmed or uninformed to apply. Dollar For's 2024 survey found 52% of patients said their
hospital gave them no information about assistance, and only 29% with unaffordable bills got charity
care. Costs: screening tools and staff time for providers, and more discounted care, which nonprofit
hospitals are expected to provide in return for tax exemption. Offsets: less spent on billing and
collecting from people who can't pay. Hospitals already reported an estimated $2.7 billion in bad debt
from likely-eligible patients (KFF). The size of net cost or savings needs a pilot to measure; we
found no published evaluation.

## Trade-offs and risks

- **Privacy.** Data checks must be limited to eligibility, with patient notice, and never used for
  marketing or collections.
- **Errors.** Automated screens can wrongly mark people ineligible, so a screen should only
  ever say "yes" automatically; a "no" must still leave a full application open.
- **Cost pressure on small and rural hospitals,** which could need state support.
- **For-profit providers and independent practices** are outside 501(r) and would only be covered by
  state law.
