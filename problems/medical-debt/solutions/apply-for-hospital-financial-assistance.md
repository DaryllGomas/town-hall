# Apply for the hospital's financial assistance (charity care), even after the bill is in collections

## Summary

Every nonprofit hospital in the U.S. must have a written financial assistance policy that gives free or
discounted care to patients who meet its income rules, and must cap what eligible patients are charged.
Many people who qualify never apply because nobody told them. Applying can erase or cut a hospital bill,
stop collection actions, and in some cases get money back and negative credit entries removed.

## How it works

1. **Find the policy.** Federal rules (IRS section 501(r)) require each nonprofit hospital to post its
   financial assistance policy, application form and a plain-language summary on its website, free to
   view without an account, and to put a notice about it on billing statements. Search the hospital's
   name plus "financial assistance policy."
2. **Check the income limits.** Hospitals set their own limits. In a 2018 look at nonprofit hospitals,
   about two-thirds offered free care above 200% of the federal poverty level, and many offer discounts
   up to 400% or more (KFF). Some states set minimums: Maryland requires free care at or below 200% of
   poverty.
3. **Apply in writing**, with whatever documents the policy lists (pay stubs, a tax return, a benefits
   letter). The hospital can't deny you for missing information its policy didn't ask for.
4. **Know the clock.** The hospital must accept and process applications for 240 days from the first bill
   after discharge, and can't take "extraordinary collection actions" (selling the debt, reporting to
   credit bureaus, suing, liens, wage garnishment) for at least 120 days. If you apply, it must pause
   those actions until it decides.
5. **If approved,** the hospital must refund anything you paid above your new amount (unless under $5)
   and take reasonable steps to reverse collection actions, including removing credit report entries and
   vacating judgments.
6. **Past the 240 days?** Apply anyway and ask in writing whether the policy allows late applications.
   Many people reach approval after a bill has gone to collections.

Free help: the nonprofit Dollar For prepares and submits applications for patients at no charge
(dollarfor.org). Hospital patient advocates and financial counselors can also help.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** The IRS enforces section 501(r) as a condition of hospitals' tax exemption; it could
  audit more hospitals' notice and screening practices and publish results. States can set minimum
  eligibility levels (as Maryland does), extend the rules to for-profit hospitals, and require reporting
  of how many patients were screened and approved.
- **People and companies with means:** Fund nonprofit application helpers like Dollar For so every
  hospital region has one. Hospitals can print the eligibility table on the front of every bill and
  staff a financial counselor at discharge. Employers can add a patient-advocate benefit that files these applications for workers.
- **You, this week:** Find your hospital's policy online. Call billing and say: "I'd like to apply for
  financial assistance under your policy. Please put my account on hold while it's reviewed and send me
  the application." Mail or upload it with proof of income, keep copies, and write down the date and the
  name of everyone you speak with. If a collector calls, tell them you've applied for the hospital's
  financial assistance and ask them to send the account back to the hospital for review.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps anyone with a nonprofit hospital bill and income under the hospital's limits, including insured
people with large deductibles if the policy covers them. For the patient it costs nothing but time.
Decisions take weeks to months. The cost lands on nonprofit hospitals, which are tax-exempt in part
in return for this kind of community benefit. KFF reported hospital
charity care costs averaged 2.6% of operating expenses in 2020.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Section 501(r) covers nonprofit hospitals only. For-profit and government hospitals, doctor groups,
  ambulances and dental offices may have no policy unless a state requires one.
- A policy may not cover every doctor who billed you for the same visit. Each policy must list which
  providers it covers and which it doesn't; check that list.
- Applications can be slow or wrongly denied; ask for the decision in writing and its basis, which the
  hospital must give.
- Moving a bill onto a credit card or medical credit card adds interest, and under most state laws the
  debt then loses medical-debt protections (Commonwealth Fund review, 2026). The CFPB advises asking
  for a payment plan instead. Apply for assistance before paying.
