# Medical debt

People in debt because of health problems: bills after an emergency or an illness, accounts sent to
collections, damaged credit, and choosing between care and rent. Many did everything right and still
went under. (Prices and insurance access are covered in [health-care-costs](../health-care-costs/).
This problem is about the debt itself and getting out from under it.)

## In people's words

> I've spent years doing everything I could possibly do and I still can't afford my own medical bills or my own home. There's a disbalance somewhere.

> I need help paying an electric bill and a late doctor bill that's pretty large.

> Hmm...go to doc tomorrow and either get assistance and make myself short on rent...or go, not get assistance

## What we know

- **It's large.** People in the U.S. owe at least $220 billion in medical debt. About 14 million adults
  (6%) owe more than $1,000, and about 3 million (1%) owe more than $10,000 (KFF and Peterson Center
  analysis of the Census Bureau's 2021 Survey of Income and Program Participation, published February
  2024). https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/the-burden-of-medical-debt-in-the-united-states/
- **It hits people who are already sick or disabled hardest.** In the same data, 13% of adults with a
  disability reported medical debt, compared with 6% of adults without one. Black adults (13%) reported
  it more often than White adults (8%). Insurance helps but doesn't prevent it: 8% of adults insured all
  year had medical debt, and 14% of adults uninsured for part of the year did.
  https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/the-burden-of-medical-debt-in-the-united-states/
- **People cut back on basics to pay.** In a 2022 KFF survey using a broader definition (including
  medical bills on credit cards and payment plans), 41% of adults had debt from medical or dental bills.
  Of those, 63% cut spending on food, clothing or household basics, and 48% used up all or most of their
  savings. https://www.kff.org/health-costs/kff-health-care-debt-survey/
- **Many people with hospital debt qualify for free or discounted care but never get it.** Nonprofit
  hospitals must have a written financial assistance policy and publicize it (IRS rules under section
  501(r)). Nonprofit hospitals themselves estimated that about $2.7 billion of the bad debt they reported
  in 2019 came from patients likely eligible for charity care (KFF, 2022). A 2024 study by the nonprofit
  Dollar For estimated a gap of at least $14 billion a year, and its survey of over 1,600 patients found
  that only 29% of people with hospital bills they couldn't afford managed to learn about, apply for and
  receive charity care; 52% said their hospital gave them no information about it.
  https://www.kff.org/health-costs/issue-brief/hospital-charity-care-how-it-works-and-why-it-matters/
  https://clearhealthcosts.com/blog/2024/05/looking-for-hospital-financial-aid-good-luck-and-dont-hold-your-breath/
- **Credit reports: no federal ban, partial voluntary limits, a patchwork of state laws (as of October
  2026).** Since 2022–2023 the three national credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) voluntarily
  leave off paid medical collections, wait one year before reporting unpaid ones, and leave off medical
  collections under $500. The CFPB found the share of consumers with a medical collection on their
  report fell from about 14% to about 5% between March 2022 and June 2023. A CFPB rule finalized in
  January 2025 to remove medical bills from credit reports was **vacated by a federal court on July 11,
  2025**, and the CFPB's own page now marks it as reference only. As of an October 16, 2026 update citing
  the National Consumer Law Center's tracker, 15 states and Washington, D.C. bar or limit medical debt on
  credit reports; industry lawsuits against the Colorado and Minnesota laws were pending, and a federal
  judge blocked a narrower Texas law on August 10, 2026. The voluntary bureau policies don't bind other
  companies that sell data to lenders.
  https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/recent-changes-in-medical-collections-on-consumer-credit-records/
  https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-finalizes-rule-to-remove-medical-bills-from-credit-reports/
  https://www.moneygeek.com/insurance/health/medical-debt-credit-reporting-bans-by-state/
  https://www.checkbook.org/national/consumers-notebook/articles/Federal-Judge-Kills-CFPB-Rule-that-Banned-Medical-Debt-in-Credit-Reports-7678
- **Wiping out old debt is cheap but isn't a cure by itself.** Illinois says its program with the
  nonprofit Undue Medical Debt erased more than $1.4 billion in medical debt for almost 700,000 residents
  using under $10 million in state funds (October 2026). But a randomized study of $169 million in
  relief for 83,401 people (published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025) found only modest
  credit gains and no measurable improvement in mental or physical health or financial wellness. Relief
  for debt already in collections works best alongside steps that stop new debt from forming.
  https://www.nprillinois.org/illinois/2026-10-15/illinois-medical-debt-relief-program-surpasses-1b-goal-in-two-years
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w32315

## Open questions

- Which hospitals and health systems screen every patient for financial assistance automatically, and
  what happened to their bad debt and collections?
- How much medical debt now sits on medical credit cards and loans, where state and hospital protections
  usually no longer apply?
- Do the pending lawsuits against state credit-reporting bans (Colorado, Minnesota) change what states
  can do? Track the outcomes.
- What helps people with debt from ambulances, physician groups and dental offices, which most hospital
  rules don't cover?
- What would actually improve health and stability for people with medical debt, given that relief
  alone showed limited effects in the one large randomized study?
