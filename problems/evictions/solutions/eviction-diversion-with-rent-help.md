# Eviction diversion: mediation plus back-rent money before a case is filed

## Summary

An eviction filing hurts a renter even if the case is later dropped, because it lands on screening
reports. Eviction diversion requires landlords to try mediation and rent assistance for a set number
of days before they can file. Philadelphia's program shows that the combination of an agreement and
money for back rent works best.

## How it works

- Before filing for nonpayment, the landlord enrolls in the city program and waits a set period
  (45 days in Philadelphia while pandemic rent money was available, 30 days after).
- The tenant meets a housing counselor, then a trained mediator helps landlord and tenant agree on a
  payment plan, a move-out date or repairs.
- Rent assistance pays some or all of the back rent. Since 2023 Philadelphia has offered "Targeted
  Financial Assistance" that pays landlords when the tenant owes less than $3,000.
- Results from the independent evaluation (Reinvestment Fund, 2023) of Philadelphia's 2021 phase:
  among tenants who got rental assistance, only 7.4% faced an eviction filing within six months,
  against 42.2% for those who didn't. Cases with an agreement had a 13.3% filing rate, against 34.7%
  overall.
- Without enough money and mediators, results drop. In 2022, after rental assistance closed and
  enrollment quadrupled, only 7% of cases got a mediation and the six-month filing rate rose to 55.6%.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Cities and counties can pass a pre-filing diversion rule like Philadelphia's, run it
  through the court, and fund a standing back-rent fund for small arrears. States can use their
  housing trust funds for it. Congress put $46.6 billion into Emergency Rental Assistance during the
  pandemic; the Eviction Lab found the money mostly reached the neighborhoods with the most evictions,
  and programs that paid tenants directly moved money faster. A smaller permanent version could be
  built on that.
- **People and companies with means:** Philanthropists, banks and local foundations can capitalize a
  "small arrears" fund that pays off debts under a few thousand dollars, where the evaluation shows
  money matters most. Employers can offer emergency grants or zero-interest loans for rent. Large
  landlords and property managers can adopt diversion voluntarily, offering a payment plan and a
  referral before any filing.
- **You, this week:** If you're behind on rent but no case has been filed, call 211 or search your
  city or county name plus "rental assistance" and apply now, since payments can take weeks. At the
  same time, ask your landlord in writing for a repayment plan; the CFPB's renter pages have steps for
  starting that conversation. If your city has an eviction diversion program, ask to be enrolled.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps renters who fell behind because of a short crisis (job loss, medical bill, car repair) and
  landlords who would rather be paid than go to court.
- Costs are the program staff, mediators and the rent money itself. Philadelphia distributed $300
  million in rental assistance during the pandemic; a permanent program focused on arrears under
  $3,000 can be much smaller.
- Paid by city, state and federal budgets, with room for private funds. Works in weeks, faster than a
  court case.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Waiting periods delay landlords who are owed money; small landlords feel this most.
- When demand outgrows mediators and funds, diversion becomes a delay without a solution, as
  Philadelphia's 2022 numbers show.
- It doesn't help tenants whose rent is simply higher than they can pay long term; they need cheaper
  housing or ongoing aid.
