# A free lawyer in eviction court (right to counsel)

## Summary

In New York City before its law, only 1% of tenants in eviction cases had a lawyer, while more than
90% of landlords did. "Right to counsel" laws guarantee eligible tenants a free lawyer. Five states, 17 cities and one county passed these laws
between 2017 and 2024. Anyone facing eviction can look for free legal aid today, even where no such law
exists yet.

## How it works

- A city, county or state passes a law that gives tenants below an income limit a free lawyer in
  eviction cases, and pays nonprofit legal aid groups to do the work.
- Court papers and notices tell tenants about the right, so they know to ask before their hearing.
- The lawyer checks whether the notice and the amount claimed are correct, raises defenses (repairs,
  wrong amounts, missed steps), and negotiates time to move or a payment plan, often connecting the
  tenant to rent help.
- New York City was first (2017). Its law gives full representation to households below 200% of the
  federal poverty level and brief advice to others. Funding for tenant legal services rose from about
  $6 million in FY13 to $166 million in FY22.
- Results reported by the city: tenant representation in housing court rose from 1% in 2013 to 71% by
  the end of FY21, and 84% of tenants who had city-funded lawyers in FY21 kept their homes. The city
  also reports evictions fell 41% from 2014 to 2019. These are the city's own figures, not a controlled
  study.
- Capacity is the hard part. The Eviction Lab reports that in practice roughly a third of New York City
  cases have received representation, because the program has never been fully funded.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** City councils and state legislatures can pass a right-to-counsel law, starting with
  the groups most at risk (families with children, older tenants), as Akron, Ohio did in its 2025
  pilot. Courts can require eviction notices to say, in plain words, where to get a free lawyer.
  Congress can keep funding the Legal Services Corporation, whose grantees spend about 40% of their
  cases on housing.
- **People and companies with means:** Philanthropists and community foundations can fund a
  right-to-counsel pilot in one county court for three years and pay for an independent evaluation, so
  the local government sees the results before taking it over. Law firms can commit pro bono hours
  every week to the local housing court. Employers can give workers a free legal-help benefit that
  covers housing cases. Large landlords can agree to pause a filing while a tenant's legal aid
  application is pending.
- **You, this week:** If you have an eviction notice or court papers, go to LawHelp.org, pick your
  state and find a free legal aid group, or use the Legal Services Corporation's "Get legal help"
  locator by entering your address. Call them the same day and say when your court date is. Then go to
  court: the CFPB warns that too many renters give up before their hearing, and responding to the case
  is how you raise defenses or get more time.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps low-income tenants facing eviction, especially families with children and older renters, and
  landlords who get a settlement or payment plan instead of a vacancy.
- Cost depends on the size of the court. New York City spent $166 million in FY22 for the largest
  program in the country. Smaller cities can start with a pilot limited to certain zip codes or groups.
- Paid by city and state budgets, federal legal aid money and private donations. A lawyer can help
  within days of a filing, but building a full program takes years of hiring.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Not enough lawyers: a guaranteed right is only as good as its funding. Underfunded programs reach a
  fraction of eligible tenants.
- Cases can take longer, which costs landlords time and money, and some may raise screening standards
  as a result.
- A lawyer can't create money. Where the tenant simply can't pay, the best outcome may be more time
  and a clean move-out, not staying.
