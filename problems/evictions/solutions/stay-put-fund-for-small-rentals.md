# A stay-put fund for renters when a building is sold or taxes jump

## Summary

Good tenants lose homes when an owner sells or when property taxes rise faster than the rent can
cover. A local "stay-put fund", backed by public, philanthropic and investor money, would get first
notice when a small rental is sold and could buy it at market price to keep current tenants housed at
stable rents. For owners who want to keep the building, it would offer a tax deal: property-tax relief
in exchange for capped rent increases.

## How it works

- **The problem it targets:** A Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia study found landlords pass $0.50
  to $0.89 of every $1 of a property tax increase on to renters. Sales are another trigger: a new owner
  may raise rents or empty the building.
- **First notice:** The city or county passes a "right of first offer" rule for small rental buildings
  (for example 1 to 20 units): when the owner lists or accepts an offer, tenants and the stay-put fund
  get notice and a short window (say 30 days) to match it. This is a lighter version of Washington,
  D.C.'s Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act, under which the city's housing agency has helped preserve
  more than 1,000 affordable units since 2002.
- **Buy and hold:** The fund buys the building at market price and transfers it to a nonprofit,
  community land trust or tenant co-op. Current tenants stay with rent increases capped (for example
  at inflation) and no evictions without cause. Rents cover operating costs and debt; the fund's
  capital comes back when the building is refinanced, and it lends again.
- **Tax deal for owners who stay:** Owners of small rentals who sign a 5-year rent-increase cap get a
  property-tax abatement or freeze, so a reassessment doesn't turn into a rent spike.
- **What's new:** Combining acquisition money, first notice and a tax deal in one local office aimed
  at owners of small buildings, who may have little room to absorb a sudden tax increase.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** City councils or county boards pass the first-offer notice rule and authorize the
  tax abatement. The local housing agency hosts the fund, as D.C.'s Department of Housing and Community
  Development does for its tenant purchase program. States can let local assessors offer the
  abatement and seed the fund from housing trust funds.
- **People and companies with means:** Philanthropists and community foundations can put in first-loss
  capital so banks and impact investors will lend the rest at low rates. Large employers can invest in
  the fund where their workers rent, as a way to keep their workforce housed. Banks can lend to the fund
  as part of their community development lending. Wealthy individuals can give or lend through a local
  community development financial institution. Landlords who are retiring can offer their building to
  the fund first.
- **You, this week:** If your landlord says they are selling or raising rent because of taxes, ask in
  writing for the sale timeline and the new rent, and check with a free legal aid group (LawHelp.org)
  whether your city or state gives tenants notice or purchase rights. Contact your local housing
  agency or community land trust to ask whether they buy buildings, and talk to your neighbors: a
  tenant group has more options than a single household.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps renters in small buildings facing sale or tax-driven increases, and small owners who want an
  exit or relief without displacing tenants.
- Costs: acquisition capital (which mostly returns over time), staff to run the fund, and lost
  property-tax revenue from abatements, which other taxpayers cover. Exact costs depend on local prices
  and need local modeling; a pilot in one city can test it.
- Paid by local and state budgets, philanthropy and investors. The tax deal can work within one tax
  year; purchases take months.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Sellers may get fewer bids or wait longer to close; the notice window must be short and the fund must
  pay full market price.
- A fund can only buy a small share of buildings; it has to prioritize where displacement risk is
  highest.
- Tax abatements shift costs to other taxpayers and need enforcement so owners keep the rent cap.
- Nonprofit and co-op owners need reserves and good management, or buildings can decline.
