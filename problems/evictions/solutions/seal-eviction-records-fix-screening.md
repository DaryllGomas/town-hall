# Seal eviction records and fix tenant screening reports

## Summary

A single eviction filing can block someone from renting for years, even if they won the case, because
screening companies copy court records into reports landlords rely on. States can seal eviction
records, as California and Colorado do from the start of a case. Renters can already get a free copy
of a screening report that cost them an apartment and dispute errors.

## How it works

- **Sealing:** Over twenty states and Washington, D.C. have some record-sealing or expungement law, but
  most only cover certain cases (a minor named in the filing, cases older than about seven years,
  dismissals, settlements or tenant wins), and many require the tenant to petition. Only California
  and Colorado keep records out of public view from the moment of filing. Massachusetts and Illinois
  automatically seal or expunge a record when the complaint names a minor.
- **Why timing matters:** Screening companies scrape court data quickly, so a record sealed later can
  already be in their files. The Eviction Lab's review finds the earlier a record is sealed, the
  greater the benefit for housing stability.
- **Screening rules:** Colorado, Minnesota, New York, Washington, Minneapolis, St. Paul and
  Philadelphia limit how screening reports can be used, and some effectively stop landlords from
  denying an applicant based on a past eviction alone.
- **Federal rights that already exist:** Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, a landlord who denies you
  because of a screening report must say so and name the company. You can get a free copy from that
  company if you ask within 60 days, and dispute errors. Evictions generally can't be reported after
  seven years, and sealed or expunged cases shouldn't appear at all.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** State legislatures can pass automatic sealing at filing, with the record opened only
  if the landlord wins a judgment, and automatic sealing of dismissed, settled and tenant-won cases.
  State court systems can make case files unavailable to non-parties from the day of filing by court
  rule. Exceptions can allow verified researchers to use anonymized data, as D.C. law does. Federal
  agencies (CFPB and FTC) can keep enforcing accuracy rules for screening companies.
- **People and companies with means:** Large landlords and property managers can adopt written
  policies not to reject applicants for filings that were dismissed or won, or that are older than a
  few years, and to use current income and payment history instead. Legal tech funders and
  philanthropists can pay for free "record clinics" and simple online tools that file sealing
  petitions in every state where they're allowed. Screening companies can verify identity with full
  date of birth before reporting a case.
- **You, this week:** If a landlord turned you down because of a screening report, ask for the
  company's name and request your free copy (within 60 days). Check it for dismissed cases shown
  without the outcome, duplicates, cases that aren't yours, or anything older than seven years, and
  dispute each error in writing with the screening company, using the CFPB's "Review your rental
  background check" guide. Then ask a free legal aid group (find one at LawHelp.org) whether your state
  lets you petition to seal the case.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps the millions of renters with an eviction filing on their record, including children named in
  filings and people wrongly matched by name.
- Sealing laws cost little to government: mostly court clerk time and computer changes. Disputing a
  report is free.
- Results show up when people next apply for housing; a corrected report can help within weeks.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Landlords lose information they use to judge risk and may lean harder on deposits, income
  requirements or credit scores instead.
- Sealing at filing reduces public visibility into court activity; research access exceptions are
  needed so patterns can still be studied.
- Records already copied by screening companies may persist unless the law requires them to purge
  sealed cases.
