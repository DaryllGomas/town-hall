# Housing affordability

## The issue

Housing costs have outrun pay: a typical home now costs about 4.7 times the typical household's
income, against 3.2 in the 1990s, and the average renter earns about $10 an hour less than it takes to
afford a modest two-bedroom apartment. The country is short an estimated 1.2 to 5.5 million homes, the
cheapest rentals are disappearing, and building costs have risen 40% since 2020, so new homes come in priced
above what most people can pay. First-time buyers have fallen to a record-low share of the market, and
half of all renters spend 30% or more of their income on housing. Renters with the lowest incomes are
hit hardest: 83% of those earning under $30,000 are cost-burdened.

## The solution

**Make it legal, fast and affordable to build modest homes everywhere, and get the help that already
exists into the hands of renters and first-time buyers.**

1. **Cities and counties** should allow apartments near transit and jobs, smaller lots and town houses,
   and end parking mandates, as Minneapolis and Houston did. ([evidence](solutions/allow-more-homes-zoning-reform.md))
2. **States** should require every city to approve backyard homes that meet clear rules, and state
   housing finance agencies should lend owners the money to build them. ([evidence](solutions/legalize-and-finance-backyard-homes-adus.md))
3. **Cities and states** should guarantee permits for small projects: pre-approved designs, a 30-day
   review clock, and fee refunds when it's missed. ([evidence](solutions/small-home-permit-guarantee.md))
4. **Employers, lenders and philanthropy** should fund down-payment help and housing counselors, and
   make sure every first-time applicant hears about local programs. ([evidence](solutions/find-down-payment-help-and-housing-counselor.md))
5. **Builders and community lenders** should build and finance the town houses, small apartments and
   backyard homes the new rules allow. ([evidence](solutions/allow-more-homes-zoning-reform.md))

## What we know

- **Homes cost nearly five times income.** In 2025 the median existing single-family home sold for 4.7
  times the median household income, down slightly from a record 5.0 in 2022 but up from 4.1 in 2019
  and an average of 3.2 in the 1990s. The income needed to afford the median-priced home was $120,800
  at the end of 2025, up from $68,700 five years earlier. Home prices rose 54% nationwide since 2020.
  ([Harvard JCHS Key Facts, 2026](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_State_Nations_Housing_2026_Key_Facts_0.pdf);
  [JCHS press release, 2026](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_State_of_the_Nations_Housing_2026_Press_Release.pdf))
- **Half of renters are stretched, and a quarter pay more than half their income.** A record 22.7
  million renter households (49%) spent 30% or more of their income on housing in 2024, including 12.1
  million (26%) who spent more than half. Among renters earning under $30,000, 83% were cost-burdened,
  and the money left after rent and utilities fell 48% from 2019 to 2024, to $210 a month.
  ([Harvard JCHS Key Facts, 2026](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_State_Nations_Housing_2026_Key_Facts_0.pdf))
- **Pay doesn't cover rent.** In 2025 a full-time worker needed $33.63 an hour to afford a modest
  two-bedroom rental at HUD's Fair Market Rent, and $28.17 for a one-bedroom. The average renter
  earned $23.60. A minimum-wage worker would need to work 116 hours a week for the two-bedroom.
  ([NLIHC Out of Reach, 2025](https://nlihc.org/sites/default/files/oor/2025_OOR_FullReport.pdf))
- **First-time buyers are being shut out.** First-time buyers were a record-low 21% of home buyers in
  the year to June 2025, and their median age hit a record 40. Their share has halved since 2007.
  ([National Association of Realtors, 2025](https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40))
- **Too few homes, and the cheapest are vanishing.** Estimates of the national housing shortage range
  from 1.2 million to 5.5 million homes. The number of units renting for under $1,000 a month (in
  today's dollars) fell by over 30% from 2014 to 2024, while units renting for $2,000 or more doubled.
  In 2024, 11.0 million extremely low-income renter households competed for 3.8 million affordable
  and available units. ([Harvard JCHS Key Facts, 2026](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_State_Nations_Housing_2026_Key_Facts_0.pdf))
- **Building costs more.** Combined prices of residential construction inputs rose 40% from January
  2020, and 62% of builders list lot availability as a significant challenge. The median asking rent
  for a newly built unit in 2025 was $1,900, against the $1,400 a median-income renter could afford.
  ([Harvard JCHS Key Facts, 2026](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_State_Nations_Housing_2026_Key_Facts_0.pdf))
- **Where rules changed, supply grew and rents held.** After Minneapolis ended parking mandates and
  allowed apartments along transit corridors, its housing stock grew 12% from 2017 to 2022 while rents
  rose 1%; in the rest of Minnesota the stock grew 4% and rents rose 14%. This is a comparison, not a
  controlled study. ([Pew, 2024](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2024/01/04/minneapolis-land-use-reforms-offer-a-blueprint-for-housing-affordability))
