# Legalize and finance backyard homes (accessory dwelling units)

## Summary

An accessory dwelling unit (ADU) is a small second home on a lot that already has a house: a backyard
cottage, a garage conversion or a basement apartment. When California required cities to approve them
without discretionary hearings and limited parking rules, permits rose from about 6,000 in 2018 to
over 15,000 in 2019. The next barrier is money, so states and lenders need to finance them.

## How it works

- **Legalize them.** California's SB 1069 (2016) required cities to adopt ADU rules meeting state
  standards, including ministerial approval (staff approve plans that meet the rules, without a
  hearing) and limits on parking requirements. Three more laws in 2019 (AB 68, AB 881, AB 670) removed
  further barriers.
- **Results** (Terner Center, UC Berkeley, 2020): ADU permits in California's largest metros rose from
  654 in 2016 to 3,126 in 2017; Los Angeles alone went from 80 to 1,980. Statewide permits rose from
  5,911 in 2018 to 15,571 in 2019, and completions more than tripled, from 1,984 to 6,668.
- ADUs were not only built in wealthy areas: almost 3,800 were completed in 2018 and 2019 in
  higher-resource areas and over 5,000 in lower-resource areas.
- **Finance them.** The average California ADU cost about $167,000 ($148,000 in Los Angeles, $237,000 in
  the Bay Area). Most owners pay with cash, a home equity line or a cash-out refinance, which shuts out
  lower-income owners. A 2020 survey of California cities found lack of financing was the top barrier.
- Small public programs show the model: Santa Cruz County offered forgivable loans up to $40,000 to
  owners who rent an ADU at an affordable rent for 20 years; Boston set aside $650,000 for no-interest
  ADU loans up to $30,000; Los Angeles piloted construction loans for owners who rent to voucher
  holders for at least five years.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States can require every city to approve ADUs that meet clear standards within a
  set time, with no parking mandate and modest fees. State housing finance agencies can offer ADU
  construction loans or forgivable loans tied to an affordable rent. Cities can publish free
  pre-approved ADU designs.
- **People and companies with means:** Banks, credit unions and community development lenders can
  offer construction loans that count the future rent toward the borrower's income. Foundations and
  employers can seed a local revolving loan fund. Builders can offer fixed-price ADU packages.
- **You, this week:** If you own a house, search your city name plus "ADU" to find the local rules,
  fees and any pre-approved plans, and ask the planning counter whether your lot qualifies. If you
  rent, search local listings for "ADU", "casita" or "in-law unit"; they are often smaller and priced
  below full apartments.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps renters looking for smaller, lower-cost homes in established neighborhoods; older owners who
  want a caregiver or relative close by; and owners who need rental income to keep their home.
- Legalizing ADUs costs the public little. Loan programs cost what the fund lends; loans come back
  unless they are forgiven in exchange for affordable rents. Each unit is privately financed and takes
  months to a year to build.

## Trade-offs and risks

- ADUs add homes one at a time; they help but cannot meet the whole shortage.
- At an average of $148,000 to $237,000 each depending on the California region, they are out of
  reach for many owners without financing, so gains go first to owners with equity or cash.
- Some ADUs could become short-term rentals instead of long-term homes unless local rules prevent it.
- Neighbors may raise concerns about parking and privacy.
