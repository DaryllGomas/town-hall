# A small-home permit guarantee: pre-approved plans, a 30-day clock and fee refunds

## Summary

Even where zoning allows modest homes, slow and uncertain permitting adds months of interest and risk,
which small builders and homeowners can least afford. This idea bundles three tools that each exist
somewhere, but rarely together: a free catalog of pre-approved designs, a guaranteed review deadline,
and a fee refund plus private review when the city misses it. It applies to projects of 1 to 10 homes.

## How it works

- **What's new:** cities or states would offer one written guarantee for small housing projects,
  combining pieces now tested separately, and publish how many days each permit actually takes.
- **Pre-approved plans.** The city pays architects for a set of designs (narrow-lot house, duplex,
  cottage court, townhouse, ADU, small apartment) that are approved in advance. A builder who uses one
  only needs a site review. South Bend, Indiana issued 91 permits under its program from January 2023;
  its narrow-lot design accounts for about 40% of them (American Planning Association).
- **A review clock.** The city must act on a complete application within a set time, such as 30 days.
  Georgia's SB 447, signed in May 2026 and in effect since July 1, 2026, requires local governments to
  act on complete applications within set timeframes and to refund all permit fees when they miss the
  deadline; applicants can also go to court to compel action.
- **Private review as a backstop.** If the city can't meet the clock, the applicant may hire a licensed
  private plan checker, and the city has a short window to issue the permit or list what doesn't
  comply. California's AB 253, taking effect in 2026, does this for residential projects of 10 units or
  fewer and under 40 feet tall, with a 30-day trigger and a 10-day city response.
- **A public scoreboard.** The city posts median days from application to permit, by project type,
  every month, so councils and residents can see whether the guarantee is kept.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** City councils can adopt the plan catalog and clock by ordinance and staff it.
  States can set the clock, fee refund and private-review backstop for every city, as Georgia and
  California have done in parts. The federal government could fund plan catalogs; the American Planning
  Association notes pending federal legislation that may do so.
- **People and companies with means:** Local architects can donate or discount designs for the
  catalog. Builders' groups and community foundations can pay for the first set of plans and for a
  test of how much time and money they save. Community lenders can pre-underwrite loans for catalog
  designs so small builders get faster financing.
- **You, this week:** If you own a lot or are thinking about adding a unit, search your city name plus
  "pre-approved plans" or "permit-ready plans" and ask the permit office what its current review
  times are. If your city has no catalog, send that question to your council member; tracking review
  times is the first step.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps small builders, homeowners adding a unit, and the renters and first-time buyers who would live
  in those homes. Faster, more predictable permits lower carrying costs at a time when construction
  inputs cost 40% more than in January 2020 (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2026).
- Costs: designing a plan catalog and hiring review staff or contract reviewers. Refunds cost the city
  only if it misses its own deadline. Exact costs need local estimates.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Pre-approved plans don't help without zoning that allows the homes and land to build on; South
  Bend's planning director said "Pre-approved plans aren't a silver bullet to your community's
  housing crisis." Builders used to larger homes may be slow
  to use them.
- Tight clocks can pressure reviewers to miss safety problems; private reviewers must be licensed and
  audited.
- Fee refunds can shrink permit budgets in understaffed cities unless the state helps fund staff.
- No independent evaluation yet measures how much time or money these tools save; a pilot should
  measure it from the start.
