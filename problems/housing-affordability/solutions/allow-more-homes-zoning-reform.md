# Allow more homes: legalize apartments near transit, smaller lots and no parking mandates

## Summary

In most places it is illegal to build anything but a detached house on most residential land, and
rules on lot size and parking add cost to every home. Minneapolis and Houston changed those rules and
got more homes. From 2017 to 2022 Minneapolis grew its housing stock 12% while rents rose 1%; in the
rest of Minnesota the stock grew 4% and rents rose 14%.

## How it works

- **Minneapolis** (Pew, 2024): the city removed parking minimums downtown in 2009, cut them near
  transit in 2015 and ended them citywide in 2021 (one national study Pew cites puts each underground
  parking space at $50,000 per unit); added "corridor" districts allowing three-to-six-story buildings along most transit routes
  and "transit" districts allowing 10-to-30-story buildings next to light rail and bus rapid transit;
  set minimum heights downtown and on major corridors; and allowed duplexes and triplexes on all
  residential lots (the Minneapolis 2040 plan took effect in 2020).
- Nearly 21,000 homes were permitted from 2017 to 2022. Pew estimates 87% were in buildings with 20 or
  more units and only 1% in two-to-four-unit buildings, so the apartment rules did most of the work and
  the triplex rule has had a modest effect so far.
- Pew estimates Minneapolis renters pay about $1,700 a year less than if rents had grown at the rest of
  the state's rate. Homelessness in Hennepin County fell 12% from 2017 to 2022 while it rose 14% in the
  rest of Minnesota. This is a before-and-after comparison, not a controlled study.
- **Houston** (Pew brief on a UT Austin study, 2023): in 1998 the city cut the minimum lot size in its
  urban core from 5,000 to 3,500 square feet (1,400 square feet with open space), and in 2013 extended
  the rule to nearly the whole city. Blocks could vote to opt out.
- At least 34,000 town houses were built from 2007 to 2020. Town houses built on former single-house
  lots had a median assessed value of $340,000, against $545,000 for other new single-family homes in
  the city, within reach of families earning about 105% of the area median income in 2020. Only about
  0.5% of single-family parcels were redeveloped.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** City councils and county boards can end parking minimums, allow apartments by right
  along transit and commercial corridors, and cut minimum lot sizes. States can set a statewide floor, for
  example allowing apartments near transit in every city. Federal grant programs can reward places that
  measurably add homes.
- **People and companies with means:** Builders and small developers can propose the town houses and
  mid-size apartments the new rules allow. Local banks and community development lenders can offer
  construction loans sized for small infill projects. Employers and foundations can fund the local
  housing study and public meetings that usually come before a zoning vote.
- **You, this week:** Find your city's zoning or comprehensive plan update on its planning department
  website and submit a written comment or sign up to speak, saying how many homes you want allowed near
  transit and jobs. Comments from renters and young buyers are often missing from these hearings.

## Who it helps and what it costs

- Helps renters and first-time buyers in growing metros, and workers who want to live near jobs.
- Costs little to the public budget: staff time to rewrite the code. Builders and lenders pay for the
  new homes. Rents respond over years, not months, as buildings are finished.

## Trade-offs and risks

- More traffic and construction on busy corridors; neighbors may object to taller buildings.
- New market-rate homes are rarely cheap on their own. They ease pressure on older homes over time but
  do not replace subsidies for the lowest-income renters.
- Results depend on demand, interest rates and building costs; a rule change in a slow market may add
  few homes.
- Opt-outs, as in Houston, reduce opposition but also reduce how much gets built.
