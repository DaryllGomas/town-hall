# AI transition pay funded by the companies gaining from AI

## Summary

When AI takes over work people used to do, the productivity gain shows up for the company using it, while the income loss shows up for the worker displaced. This idea connects the two: the companies gaining most from AI pay into a transition fund that tops up the wages of displaced workers who take a lower-paying job, and pays for proven training. The top-up idea is not new. The U.S. tested it for trade-displaced workers, and it helped people get back to work faster. What's new is applying it to AI and having the biggest winners pay for it.

## How it works

1. **Pay-in.** Large AI developers and the companies that cut the most jobs through automation contribute to a national transition fund. This could start voluntarily as a pledge, then become a small levy tied to AI revenue or to automation-linked layoffs.
2. **Wage top-up.** A worker who loses a job they held two or more years and takes a new full-time job at lower pay gets half the difference for two years. That is the design Brookings proposed in 2005 (capped at $10,000 a year), and it resembles the Trade Adjustment Assistance program's wage insurance for workers 50 and over.
3. **Training option.** Workers who prefer to retrain get the fund to pay for programs with randomized evidence (see the sectoral training fix in this folder), with a living stipend.
4. **Fast access.** Workers apply when they file for unemployment, with no need to prove AI caused the layoff: any long-tenure job loss qualifies, which keeps it simple and fair.

Why it could work:
- Displaced workers often take a pay cut. Of long-tenure workers displaced in 2023 to 2025 who found full-time jobs, only about 49 percent earned as much as before (BLS).
- Wage insurance gets people working sooner. Studies of the trade program's version found eligibility raised short-run employment, and a national study found it raised long-run cumulative earnings and was "self-financing even under conservative assumptions."
- The exposure is wide: the IMF estimates about 60 percent of jobs in advanced economies may be affected by AI, and the youngest workers in AI-exposed fields are already seeing fewer jobs.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** Congress can create a general displacement wage insurance program (the trade-only version stopped taking new groups of workers in July 2022) and let private contributions fund it. States can pilot it with their own funds.
- **People and companies with means:** The AI labs, cloud providers and large employers that report the biggest AI-driven savings can jointly seed the fund now, without waiting for law, for example by pledging a fixed share of AI-related revenue or of the savings from each automated role. Investors and philanthropists can fund a randomized pilot in one or two states so there's hard evidence within a few years. Any company that automates roles can offer this top-up to its own laid-off workers on its own.
- **You, this week:** If you were laid off and are weighing a lower-paying job, take the job offer seriously rather than waiting: the research shows faster re-employment pays off over time. File for unemployment first, and ask your local American Job Center whether any wage-supplement or training funds apply to your layoff.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps experienced workers whose skills lost value: older workers, mid-career white-collar staff, and people in clerical and routine jobs. Brookings estimated its version would cost roughly $3.5 billion a year net in 2005, about $25 per worker per year. Costs would rise if AI displacement grows. A levy on the largest AI beneficiaries spreads that cost to those collecting the gains. Payments start as soon as someone takes a new job.

## Trade-offs and risks

- It may push people into lower-paying jobs instead of holding out for better ones. Paying only for two years and only after re-employment limits that.
- Defining "AI gains" or "automation layoffs" for a levy is hard and could be gamed; a voluntary fund or a broad tax avoids that but raises less.
- The $10,000 cap may leave out higher earners with the biggest losses.
- A voluntary pledge can fade when markets turn.
