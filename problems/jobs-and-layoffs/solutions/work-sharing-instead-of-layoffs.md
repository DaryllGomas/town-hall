# Work-sharing: cut hours for everyone instead of laying some off

## Summary

When business slows, an employer can cut everyone's hours a little instead of laying some people off entirely. Under state "work-sharing" (short-time compensation) programs, workers collect partial unemployment benefits for the hours lost, keep their health and retirement benefits, and keep their jobs. About 30 states run these programs today, but many employers and workers have never heard of them.

## How it works

1. The employer files a work-sharing plan with the state unemployment agency for a group of workers whose hours will be cut (the employer applies, not the worker).
2. Once approved, each worker gets a share of the unemployment benefit equal to the share of hours cut. The Department of Labor's example: a worker cut from 40 to 32 hours (20 percent) who would have received $270 a week if fully laid off gets $54 in benefits on top of wages for 32 hours, plus health and retirement benefits.
3. Workers don't have to search for other jobs while on the plan, but must be available for their normal week. Many states require an unpaid waiting week first.
4. When business recovers, everyone goes back to full hours with no rehiring or retraining.

Per the Department of Labor, 30 states have operational programs, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Florida, Illinois, Michigan, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States without a program can pass one using federal model legislation. States with one can make the employer application online and simple, and tell every employer filing a layoff notice that work-sharing exists.
- **People and companies with means:** Employers facing a temporary slowdown can choose work-sharing before layoffs and keep the trained team they will need again. Larger companies can go further and top up pay so workers on reduced hours lose less. Industry associations and chambers of commerce can teach members how to file a plan.
- **You, this week:** If your hours are being cut or layoffs are rumored, look up "shared work" or "work share" on your state labor department's website, print the page and ask HR or your manager whether the company will file a plan. If you've already lost hours, ask your state unemployment agency whether you can claim partial benefits.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps hourly and salaried workers at firms facing a temporary drop in demand, and the employers who keep their staff. Benefits come from the state unemployment trust fund, which employers already pay into. A National Employment Law Project analysis notes the benefit cost of cutting 100 workers by one day a week is roughly equal to laying off 20 workers for a full week, and found no evidence work-sharing costs more than layoffs. Workers on a typical plan end up with up to about 90 percent of their usual take-home pay. It can start within weeks of an approved plan.

## Trade-offs and risks

- It suits temporary slowdowns. If jobs are disappearing permanently, for example because of automation, it only delays the layoff.
- Some subsidized hours would have been kept anyway. Research on the 2008 to 2009 recession found short-time work reduced job losses, but saved fewer jobs than the hours it paid for.
- It only works in states with a program and only if the employer applies.
- Everyone on the plan takes a pay cut, including workers who would not have been laid off.
