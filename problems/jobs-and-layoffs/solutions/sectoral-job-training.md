# Free sector-focused job training with a track record

## Summary

Most job training doesn't move earnings much. One kind does: "sectoral" programs that train people for specific jobs employers are hiring for, screen applicants carefully, and add coaching and help with bills and child care. Randomized trials of Year Up, Project QUEST and Per Scholas found earnings gains that lasted for years. Several are free to the trainee.

## How it works

- **Pick a sector with real openings** (IT support, health care, skilled trades, finance operations) and build the training with local employers so graduates fit actual jobs.
- **Screen and support.** Programs check that applicants are ready, then add counseling, remedial math and reading, and help with rent, utilities, transport or child care so people don't drop out.
- **Place people.** Internships and direct employer connections at the end.

What the evidence shows:

- **Year Up** (ages 18 to 29, about one year, tuition-free with a stipend): in a federally sponsored randomized trial run by Abt Associates, participants earned 30 percent more than the control group six years out, gains of over $8,000 a year lasted through year seven, and the updated cost-benefit estimate is $2.46 in benefits per $1 spent.
- **Project QUEST** (San Antonio, health care careers): a randomized trial of 410 mostly working, low-income adults found earnings $5,240 a year higher in year 9 and $4,616 higher in year 11 (the year-11 gain is not statistically significant). Net cost was about $8,844 per participant.
- **Per Scholas** (IT, cybersecurity, data; 3 to 4 months, zero tuition, 25+ campuses): in MDRC's WorkAdvance trial it raised earnings in multiple follow-up years.
- Across these programs, a review by Katz, Roth, Hendra and Schaberg found earnings gains of 11 to 40 percent after training.

## Who can make it happen

- **Government:** States and local workforce boards can steer more of their federal training money (WIOA) toward programs with randomized evidence, and pay for the supports (child care, transport, stipends) that keep people enrolled. Cities can link community colleges to employers in growing sectors.
- **People and companies with means:** Employers can co-design curricula, host paid internships and commit to interview graduates. Companies cutting jobs because of automation can fund cohorts for the people they let go. Philanthropists can pay for expansion to new cities. Amazon, for example, committed over $1.2 billion from 2019 through 2025 to give 300,000 employees access to free training; more employers can do this, and open it to workers they lay off.
- **You, this week:** Check whether Year Up (if you're 18 to 29) or Per Scholas has a campus or track near you and start an application; both are free. Then visit your local American Job Center (find it through your state's labor department) and ask about Dislocated Worker training funds if you were laid off.

## Who it helps and what it costs

It helps low-wage workers and people stuck after a layoff who can commit months to training. Costs per person run from a few thousand to more than $10,000 (Project QUEST's net cost was $8,844), paid by philanthropy, employers and public workforce funds. It is not fast: training takes 3 months to 2 years, and earnings gains show up after that.

## Trade-offs and risks

- Results vary a lot by program. In WorkAdvance only Per Scholas clearly raised earnings; other sites didn't.
- Screening means the people facing the biggest barriers may not get in.
- People need income during training. Stipends help, but they don't replace a full-time wage.
- Programs depend on local employer demand. If a sector stops hiring, graduates struggle.
